# How to Build Your ADHD Dopamine Menu: A Step-by-Step Template

A dopamine menu is one of those ADHD strategies that sounds almost too simple to work — until you try it and realize it's the only thing standing between you and another three-hour phone scroll.

The concept was popularized by Jessica McCabe of How to ADHD, and it's brilliant in its simplicity: you create a personalized list of activities organized by how much effort and time they require, so that when your brain is screaming for stimulation, you have something to reach for that isn't doom-scrolling, impulse shopping, or eating an entire bag of chips.

Here's how to build one that actually works for your specific brain.

## What Is a Dopamine Menu?

A dopamine menu is a curated, categorized list of activities that give your brain the dopamine it's looking for — without the crash, guilt, or wasted hours that come from unintentional dopamine-seeking behaviors.

The menu metaphor works because it mirrors how a restaurant menu functions: you don't stare at infinite possibilities, you pick from a curated set of options. For ADHD brains, which are terrible at generating options under pressure, having a pre-made list removes the executive function barrier between "I need stimulation" and "I'm doing something healthy."

### Why can't you just pick an activity in the moment?

Because choosing is itself an executive function task. When your dopamine is depleted — which is exactly the moment you need the menu — your prefrontal cortex is running on fumes. Asking it to brainstorm, evaluate, and select an activity is asking it to do the thing it currently can't do.

The dopamine menu solves this by front-loading the decision to a time when your brain IS functioning. You build the menu on a good day. You use the menu on a bad day. The bad-day version of you only has to read and pick — no creative thinking required.

## The Four Categories: Appetizers, Entrees, Sides, and Desserts

The classic dopamine menu uses a food metaphor to organize activities by intensity and type. Here's how each category works:

### Appetizers: Quick, Low-Effort Dopamine Hits (2-15 minutes)

These are your go-to activities when you need a fast boost and can't commit to anything big. They should require almost zero initiation energy — you can do them from wherever you're sitting, with whatever you have on hand.

**What makes a good appetizer:**
- Takes less than 15 minutes
- Requires no preparation or equipment
- Can be done alone, right now
- Provides an immediate (if small) dopamine bump

**Examples to consider for your menu:**
- Listen to one favorite song at full volume
- Do a 5-minute stretch
- Step outside and feel the sun or cold air on your face
- Watch one funny video (set a timer — this is key)
- Doodle, sketch, or color for 5 minutes
- Chew mint gum
- Smell essential oils (peppermint and citrus are particularly activating)
- Text a friend something you appreciate about them
- Do 10 push-ups or 20 jumping jacks
- Drink a glass of ice water
- Tidy one small surface for 5 minutes
- Read one page of a book

**Your appetizer section should have at least 8-10 options.** Variety matters because ADHD brains habituate quickly — what works on Monday might not work on Thursday.

### Entrees: Sustained, Engaging Activities (30-90 minutes)

These are your main dishes — activities that keep your brain engaged for a meaningful stretch of time and produce a deeper, longer-lasting dopamine effect. They require more initiation energy than appetizers but pay off significantly more.

**What makes a good entree:**
- Keeps you engaged for 30+ minutes
- Involves enough complexity to prevent boredom
- Produces a sense of accomplishment or flow
- Is something you genuinely enjoy (not something you think you should enjoy)

**Examples to consider for your menu:**
- Go for a hike or long walk with a podcast
- Cook a new recipe from scratch
- Work on a creative project (writing, art, music, coding)
- Play a video game with a clear goal or storyline
- Exercise — gym, run, swim, bike ride
- Deep-clean one room (the before/after dopamine is real)
- Build something (LEGO, furniture, crafts)
- Watch a documentary on a topic you know nothing about
- Practice a skill you're learning (instrument, language, sport)
- Work on a jigsaw puzzle
- Garden or do yard work
- Reorganize a closet or bookshelf

**Your entree section should have 5-7 options.** These are higher commitment, so you need fewer but they should all be things that reliably hold your attention.

### Sides: Social Activities (Any Duration)

Social dopamine is in its own category because it works differently from solitary activities. Connection with other people activates oxytocin alongside dopamine, creating a uniquely satisfying neurochemical blend. Sides can be combined with appetizers or entrees.

**What makes a good side:**
- Involves genuine human interaction (not passive scrolling of social media)
- Can be initiated without too much planning
- Leaves you feeling connected, not drained

**Examples to consider for your menu:**
- Call a friend or family member
- Have coffee with someone
- Play a board game or card game with someone in your household
- Body-double with a friend (work alongside each other, in person or on a video call)
- Go to a group fitness class
- Volunteer for a few hours
- Join a Discord server or online community related to an interest
- Co-work at a cafe or library

**Your sides section should have 4-6 options.** Include a mix of in-person and remote options so you always have something accessible.

### Desserts: High-Dopamine Treats (Use Sparingly)

These are activities that produce intense dopamine but can become problematic if overused. They're not bad — they just need boundaries. Think of them as actual desserts: enjoyable in moderation, counterproductive as a steady diet.

**What makes a dessert:**
- Produces a strong, fast dopamine rush
- Has addictive potential or can easily consume hours
- Often involves screens, spending money, or consumption
- Feels great in the moment but may leave you feeling worse after extended use

**Examples to consider for your menu:**
- Play a highly engaging video game (with a timer set)
- Online shopping browsing (with a rule: add to wishlist, don't buy for 48 hours)
- Binge a TV series (limit to 2 episodes)
- Social media scrolling (with a 15-minute timer)
- Eating a favorite treat or comfort food
- Watching YouTube rabbit holes (with a topic limit)

**Your desserts section should have 3-5 options, each with a built-in boundary.** The boundary is non-negotiable. Without it, desserts expand to fill all available time and crowd out everything else.

## Building Your Menu: The Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1: Pick a good brain day

Don't try to build your dopamine menu when you're in the middle of a shutdown. You need your executive function at least partially online to do this thoughtfully. A Saturday morning after good sleep, or an afternoon when your medication (if you take it) is at peak effectiveness, is ideal.

### Step 2: Brainstorm without filtering

Set a 15-minute timer and write down every activity that has ever made you feel good, engaged, or energized. Don't sort, don't judge, don't worry about categories. Just dump. Include things from childhood, things you haven't done in years, things that seem silly. Your brain knows what gives it dopamine — let it talk.

### Step 3: Sort into categories

Take your brainstormed list and place each activity into the appetizer, entree, side, or dessert category. If something could fit multiple categories, put it where it's most useful. A 5-minute dance break is an appetizer; a 45-minute dance session is an entree.

### Step 4: Add boundaries to desserts

For every dessert item, write a specific limit next to it. Not "don't do this too much" — that's vague and your ADHD brain will ignore it. Instead: "2 episodes max," "15 minutes with phone timer," "add to wishlist only, review in 48 hours." You can find timer and habit tracking tools on [ToolCraftKit.com](https://toolcraftkit.com) to help enforce these boundaries.

### Step 5: Check for accessibility

Look at each item and ask: "Could I do this right now, with what I have, without needing to plan?" If the answer is no, either simplify it or note what you'd need to prepare in advance. An appetizer that requires a trip to the store isn't really an appetizer.

### Step 6: Make it visible

This is where most dopamine menus fail. They get written down, filed away, and forgotten. Your menu needs to be physically present where you most often experience dopamine crashes. Options:

- Print it and tape it to your wall next to your desk
- Keep it as a note on your phone's home screen
- Write it on a whiteboard in your kitchen
- Set it as your phone or computer wallpaper
- Store it in a planner you actually open daily

## Common Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

### Putting too many "shoulds" on the menu

If your menu is full of activities you think you should enjoy — meditation, journaling, yoga — but you don't actually enjoy them, you won't use the menu. Be honest with yourself. A dopamine menu filled with genuine pleasures that happens to include some healthy options is infinitely better than a virtuous menu you never look at.

### Not rotating items

ADHD brains crave novelty. An activity that gave you a huge dopamine boost last month might feel flat this month. Review your menu every 3-4 weeks and swap out anything that's lost its appeal. Keep a "retired" list so you can bring things back later when they feel fresh again.

### Forgetting the menu exists during a crash

This is the biggest challenge. When you're deep in executive dysfunction, you might not even remember that you have a menu. Solutions: set a recurring daily alarm labeled "Check your dopamine menu." Ask a partner or roommate to suggest it when they see you struggling. Make it the first thing you see when you unlock your phone.

### Using only desserts

If you find yourself going straight to desserts every time, your appetizer section needs work. It probably doesn't contain enough genuinely appealing options, or the options require too much effort. Make your appetizers as frictionless as possible — the goal is that reaching for a dessert feels like unnecessary effort compared to trying an appetizer first.

## Making It Stick

The dopamine menu isn't magic. It's a tool — and like any tool, it only works if you actually pick it up. The first few times you use it will feel awkward. You might resist it. Your brain might argue that nothing on the list sounds appealing right now.

Do it anyway. Pick the appetizer that sounds least unappealing and try it for 2 minutes. That's all. Two minutes. If it doesn't shift your state, try another one. The act of choosing and trying is itself a micro-dose of executive function practice. Over time, reaching for the menu becomes automatic — a habit that replaces reaching for your phone.
