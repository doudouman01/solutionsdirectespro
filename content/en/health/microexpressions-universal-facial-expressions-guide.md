# Microexpressions: How to Read the 7 Universal Facial Expressions in Under a Second

In 1966, Paul Ekman traveled to Papua New Guinea to settle one of psychology's oldest debates: are facial expressions learned or universal?

He showed photographs of facial expressions to the Fore people — an isolated tribe with virtually no exposure to Western media, movies, or photographs. They identified the emotions with the same accuracy as American college students.

That study, and the decades of research that followed, established one of the most robust findings in behavioral science: **seven basic emotions produce the same facial expressions across every culture on Earth**. Whether you're in Tokyo, Lagos, São Paulo, or a pre-literate village in the highlands of New Guinea, anger looks like anger. Fear looks like fear. Contempt looks like contempt.

More importantly, these expressions are **involuntary**. They're controlled by subcortical brain structures — particularly the amygdala and the basal ganglia — that operate below conscious control. When someone feels a genuine emotion, the corresponding facial expression fires automatically.

This means two things:

1. You can learn to read these expressions reliably across any culture
2. People cannot fully suppress them — even when they try, the real expression leaks through as a **microexpression**, lasting between 1/25th and 1/5th of a second

Learning to spot microexpressions is one of the highest-return skills in body language literacy. Here's the science and the practical training framework.

## What Exactly Is a Microexpression?

A microexpression is a brief, involuntary facial expression that occurs when someone attempts to conceal or suppress a genuine emotion. It differs from a regular expression in three specific ways:

**Duration**: A normal facial expression lasts between 0.5 and 4 seconds. A microexpression lasts between 1/25th of a second (40 milliseconds) and 1/5th of a second (200 milliseconds). Some are so fast they occur within a single eye blink.

**Involuntary origin**: Regular expressions can be voluntary (a polite smile) or involuntary (a genuine smile). Microexpressions are always involuntary — they're the face's unfiltered response before the conscious mind imposes control.

**Context**: Microexpressions appear specifically when someone is trying to hide their emotional reaction. If there's no reason to conceal, the expression simply plays out normally. Microexpressions are the product of a conflict between the limbic system (which generates the emotion) and the prefrontal cortex (which tries to suppress or mask it).

## The 7 Universal Expressions: Anatomy and Recognition

Paul Ekman's Facial Action Coding System (FACS) maps every possible facial movement to specific muscle activations called Action Units (AUs). Each universal expression involves a distinct combination of AUs that you can learn to recognize.

### 1. Happiness

**The muscle signature:**
- **Zygomatic major** — pulls the lip corners up and back
- **Orbicularis oculi (pars orbitalis)** — creates crow's feet at the outer corners of the eyes and raises the cheeks

**How to spot a genuine smile vs. a fake one:**

This is the single most important distinction in facial expression reading. The Duchenne smile — named after French neurologist Guillaume Duchenne, who first documented it — engages both the mouth muscles and the muscles around the eyes. The orbicularis oculi contracts, pushing the cheeks up and creating wrinkles around the eye corners.

A social or polite smile only activates the mouth. The eyes don't change.

Here's the test: cover the bottom half of the face. If the person looks happy from the eyes alone — cheeks raised, slight squinting, crow's feet visible — the smile is genuine. If the eyes look neutral while the mouth is smiling, it's performed.

**As a microexpression:** A flash of genuine happiness that someone is trying to suppress appears as a brief crow's-feet contraction, sometimes without any visible mouth movement at all. Watch the eye corners, not the mouth.

### 2. Sadness

**The muscle signature:**
- **Inner corners of the eyebrows rise** (Action Unit 1) — this is the most reliable single indicator of sadness and one of the hardest to fake voluntarily
- **Lip corners pull downward**
- **Lower lip may push upward slightly** (the "pout")
- **Gaze drops downward**

**Why it's hard to fake:**

AU1 — the inner eyebrow raise — is controlled by the frontalis muscle (medial portion), which fewer than 15% of people can activate voluntarily. This means that when you see the inner eyebrows rise while the outer eyebrows stay relatively flat, you're almost certainly seeing genuine sadness or distress.

**As a microexpression:** A flash of sadness often appears as just the inner eyebrow raise, lasting a fraction of a second before the person's face resets to neutral. It frequently appears when someone is trying to seem fine — saying "I'm okay" while the inner eyebrows briefly telegraph the opposite.

### 3. Fear

**The muscle signature:**
- **Eyebrows raised and pulled together** (AU1+2+4) — unlike surprise, where the eyebrows raise evenly, fear pulls them together in the center
- **Upper eyelids raised** — more white is visible above the iris
- **Lower eyelids tense**
- **Mouth opens, lips stretch horizontally** — pulled back toward the ears rather than dropping down (which is surprise)

**The fear-surprise distinction:**

Fear and surprise share raised eyebrows and widened eyes, which is why people frequently confuse them. The key differences:

| Feature | Fear | Surprise |
|---------|------|----------|
| Eyebrows | Raised AND pulled together (flat/angled) | Raised AND curved (rounded) |
| Mouth | Lips stretch horizontally | Jaw drops open (round) |
| Duration | Persists or transitions to another emotion | Brief, transitions quickly |

Fear's eyebrows create a flat or inverted-V shape. Surprise's eyebrows create rounded arches.

**As a microexpression:** Fear microexpressions most commonly appear as a brief widening of the eyes with eyebrow tension — particularly in situations where someone encounters unexpected information they find threatening. In professional settings, this can flash across someone's face when they hear news that affects their position or security.

### 4. Anger

**The muscle signature:**
- **Eyebrows lower and pull together** (AU4) — creating vertical furrows between the brows
- **Upper eyelids raise while lower eyelids tense** — creating a hard stare
- **Lips press together tightly** or the mouth opens in a square shape
- **Nostrils may flare** (AU38)
- **Jaw pushes forward slightly**

**The subtlety scale:**

Anger is the expression people work hardest to suppress in professional and social settings, because displaying anger carries social penalties. This means anger microexpressions are among the most common and most valuable to read.

Micro-anger often appears as just one component — a brief lip press, a momentary brow furrow, or a nostril flare — rather than the full expression. Even a single anger component, when it contradicts the person's verbal composure, is significant.

**As a microexpression:** The most common anger microexpression in professional settings is the brief lip compression — pressing the lips together for a fraction of a second — immediately after hearing something the person disagrees with but chooses not to challenge verbally. It's so brief most people miss it, but once you know to look for it, you'll see it constantly.

### 5. Disgust

**The muscle signature:**
- **Upper lip raises** (AU10) — the levator labii superioris is the primary muscle
- **Nose wrinkles** (AU9) — contraction of the nasalis muscle
- **Cheeks raise**
- **Lower lip may push forward and down**
- **Eyebrows lower slightly**

**The moral dimension:**

Research by Jonathan Haidt and others demonstrated that the disgust expression appears not only in response to physical revolting stimuli (rotten food, bodily waste) but also in response to moral violations. When someone finds an idea, behavior, or person morally repugnant, the same facial muscles fire that would respond to a physical contaminant.

This makes disgust microexpressions particularly revealing in conversations about ethics, values, and behavior. A brief nose wrinkle during a discussion about someone's conduct reveals moral evaluation that the person may not express verbally.

**As a microexpression:** Disgust micro-expressions typically manifest as a brief nose wrinkle or upper lip raise. In conversation, watch for a quick nose wrinkle when someone discusses a person, behavior, or idea they find distasteful — it appears even when their words remain neutral or polite.

### 6. Surprise

**The muscle signature:**
- **Eyebrows raise high and curve** (AU1+2) — the curve is key; fear produces flat or angled raised brows
- **Eyes widen, more white visible** above and below the iris
- **Jaw drops open** — the mouth opens vertically into a rounded shape
- **Forehead wrinkles horizontally**

**The timing tells the truth:**

Genuine surprise is the shortest-duration universal expression — it lasts less than a second before transitioning into another emotion (fear, happiness, anger, or confusion, depending on what caused the surprise). If "surprise" lasts longer than one second, it's likely performed.

This timing rule makes surprise one of the easier expressions to verify as genuine or fake. A friend opens a surprise party — if the wide-eyed, jaw-drop expression persists for two or three seconds with maintained intensity, it's being performed for the crowd. Genuine surprise is a flash that immediately transforms.

**As a microexpression:** Surprise microexpressions are relatively rare because surprise is hard to suppress — it happens before the cortex can intervene. When they do appear, they usually indicate that someone was expecting a different outcome and is trying to act unsurprised. The flash of raised eyebrows and widened eyes betrays the genuine reaction.

### 7. Contempt

**The muscle signature:**
- **One corner of the mouth raises** (unilateral AU12) — this is the only asymmetrical universal expression
- **The corresponding cheek tightens slightly**
- **The rest of the face may remain neutral**

**Why contempt is unique:**

Contempt is the only universal expression that appears on just one side of the face. It's also the only one that doesn't correspond to a simple emotion but to a judgment — a sense of moral superiority or dismissal.

Research by John Gottman at the University of Washington found that contempt in a romantic relationship is the single strongest predictor of relationship dissolution. When one partner displays contempt toward the other — that one-sided mouth raise — the relationship is in serious trouble.

**As a microexpression:** Contempt microexpressions are subtle and extremely common in professional settings. A brief, one-sided lip raise during someone else's presentation, after hearing a suggestion, or while reading an email reveals that the person holds the source in low regard — regardless of what they say.

Because it's asymmetrical and subtle, contempt is the hardest microexpression for beginners to spot. Practice by watching panel discussions and meetings: the one-sided mouth raise appears with surprising frequency when participants disagree but maintain verbal diplomacy.

## Training Your Recognition Speed

Recognizing microexpressions requires training because the signals are faster than untrained conscious perception. Here's a structured approach:

### Phase 1: Pattern Recognition (Weeks 1-2)

Study photographs of each expression until you can identify all seven instantly. Focus on the muscle groups, not the overall "feel." You should be able to identify each expression by its anatomical components:

- Happiness = crow's feet + raised cheeks
- Sadness = inner brow raise
- Fear = brows raised AND pulled together + horizontal mouth stretch
- Anger = brow furrow + lip compression or square mouth
- Disgust = nose wrinkle + upper lip raise
- Surprise = curved brow raise + round open mouth + less than 1 second
- Contempt = unilateral mouth raise

### Phase 2: Speed Training (Weeks 3-4)

Watch video content — interviews, panel discussions, press conferences, talk shows — and practice spotting expressions in real time. Start with content where emotions are visible (debates, emotional interviews) and progress to content where people are controlling their expressions (diplomatic press conferences, corporate earnings calls).

Pause and rewind when you think you spotted something. You're training your perceptual speed.

### Phase 3: Real-World Application (Ongoing)

Begin observing microexpressions in daily interactions. Start with low-stakes situations — casual conversations, meetings where you're observing rather than participating — before using the skill in high-stakes contexts.

Remember the cluster method: a single microexpression is a data point, not a conclusion. Note it, look for confirming signals across other channels (body, voice, verbal content), and build your assessment from the full picture.

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

**Mistake 1: Over-reading.** Not every facial twitch is a microexpression. Tics, itches, and random muscle firings are noise. True microexpressions form recognizable universal expressions, not random movements.

**Mistake 2: Ignoring the baseline.** Some people's resting face includes components of universal expressions (resting frown, natural lip asymmetry). Without establishing what's normal for that person, you'll produce false reads constantly.

**Mistake 3: Confirmation bias.** If you expect someone to be lying, you'll "see" anger and fear microexpressions that aren't there. Always consider the null hypothesis: could this person simply be telling the truth?

**Mistake 4: Cultural intensity differences.** While the seven expressions are universal, their display rules — how intensely and how freely they're expressed — vary by culture. In high-context cultures (Japan, Korea, many Middle Eastern countries), emotional expression is more controlled in professional settings, producing more microexpressions but also more practiced composure.

**Mistake 5: Neglecting context.** A flash of fear during a job interview might mean the candidate is lying, or it might mean they're genuinely afraid of not getting the job. Same expression, different meaning. Context is everything.
