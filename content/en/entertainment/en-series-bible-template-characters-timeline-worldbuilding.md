# Series Bible Template: How to Track Characters, Timeline, and World-Building Across Multiple Books

By book three of your series, your world contains hundreds of named details. Character descriptions, location geography, relationship histories, revealed secrets, established rules, planted foreshadowing, unresolved subplots, timeline dependencies, and promises made to the reader through setup that demands payoff. All of this lives in your head — until it does not.

The moment you write "her blue eyes" for a character whose eyes were brown in book one, you have a problem. The moment a character references an event that has not happened yet in the timeline, you have a bigger problem. The moment a reader catches these errors in a review — and they will — you have a credibility problem that follows your series forever.

A series bible prevents all of this. It is a living reference document that captures every decision, every detail, and every thread in your series — organized so that any fact is findable in seconds during drafting. Authors who maintain series bibles write faster (because they are not searching through manuscripts), write more consistently (because the reference is always current), and receive fewer continuity complaints in reviews.

Here is how to build one.

## The character section

### What belongs in a character profile?

Every named character who appears in more than one scene needs a profile. For major characters (protagonists, antagonists, love interests, key secondary characters), the profile should be comprehensive. For minor recurring characters (the bartender who appears in every book, the protagonist's boss, the neighbor), a brief entry is sufficient.

A major character profile includes: full name and any aliases or nicknames. Physical description (detailed enough that you never contradict yourself — eye color, hair color, height, build, distinguishing features, and how these change across books). Age at each book's timeline position. Personality baseline — the core traits that define them. Backstory — what happened before the series begins that shapes their behavior. Relationships — who they are connected to and how those connections evolve. Knowledge state — what they know and when they learned it (critical for mysteries and thrillers where information reveals drive the plot). Emotional arc per book — where they start and end emotionally in each installment. Unresolved wounds, goals, and desires that carry forward.

The knowledge state entry is often overlooked and always critical. In a series with secrets, lies, and gradual revelation, keeping track of who knows what and when they learned it prevents the devastating error of a character reacting to information they have not received yet.

### How do you track character development across books?

Create a development log for each major character — a chronological record of their key emotional moments, decisions, and changes across the series. Each entry includes the book number, the scene or chapter, and a brief description of what happened and how it changed the character.

This log serves two purposes. First, it prevents regression — a character who grew in book two should not revert to their pre-growth behavior in book three without a narrative reason. Second, it provides a roadmap for future development — you can see the character's trajectory and plan where they need to go next.

The format can be as simple as a list: "Book 1, Ch. 12: Confronts father for the first time. Realizes anger is masking grief. Book 1, Ch. 22: Chooses to forgive — not for father's sake, but for her own. Book 2, Ch. 3: Forgiveness tested when father reappears. Old anger surfaces but she handles it differently now." This running log makes character consistency effortless.

## The world-building section

### How do you organize world-building for a series?

World-building entries should be organized by category, not by book. A category-based structure — locations, politics, culture, technology, magic, history, organizations, species — lets you find any detail instantly, regardless of which book introduced it.

Each category should contain two levels of information: established facts (details that have appeared on the page and are therefore canon) and background notes (details you have decided but not yet revealed, which may change before they appear in a future book). Mark these clearly. Established facts are immutable — changing them creates a continuity error. Background notes are flexible — they can evolve as the series develops.

For each location entry: name, physical description, population, cultural character, notable landmarks, which characters are associated with it, and which scenes have occurred there (with book and chapter references). For each political or organizational entry: structure, leadership, goals, resources, relationships with other factions, and how these evolve across books.

### How do you handle rules and systems?

Magic systems, technology rules, political structures, and social norms are the backbone of series consistency. A rule established in book one constrains every subsequent book. If your magic system requires a physical cost for spellcasting, a character who casts freely in book four without paying that cost has broken the reader's trust.

Document every rule explicitly, with the scene where it was established. Include edge cases you have considered but not yet used — these are future plot possibilities. And document the limitations as carefully as the capabilities. Limitations create conflict. Capabilities without limitations create boring characters.

When you need to evolve a rule (magic systems that grow, technology that advances, political structures that change), plan the evolution as a narrative event, not a retcon. The rule changes because something happens in the story — a discovery, a revolution, a sacrifice. The reader witnesses the change and understands why the rules are different now. A rule that simply changes between books with no explanation is a continuity error, regardless of your intention.

## The timeline section

### Why is a timeline tracker essential?

Timelines are the most common source of continuity errors in series, and the hardest to fix after publication. A character who was 28 in book one and 29 in book three — set two years later — has a problem. A pregnancy that lasts eleven months has a bigger one. A winter scene that follows a summer scene set "the next week" is the kind of error that eagle-eyed readers catalog and share.

Build your timeline as a chronological spreadsheet or table. Columns: date (or relative time marker), event, characters involved, location, book and chapter reference. Every significant event gets an entry. Season markers, character ages at key points, and travel time between locations should be explicitly tracked.

For series with parallel timelines (multiple POV characters experiencing events simultaneously in different locations), add a synchronization row at regular intervals: "While [Character A] is doing X in Location 1, [Character B] is doing Y in Location 2." This prevents the common error of two characters experiencing the same day at different speeds.

### How do you handle time jumps between books?

Time jumps between books — six months between book two and book three, a year between books four and five — are common and useful for series pacing. But they require explicit tracking of what happened during the gap.

Create a "gap summary" for each inter-book period: what each major character did, how relationships evolved, what world events occurred, and what the reader needs to know when book three opens. This summary does not need to appear in the text — it informs how you write the opening chapters, ensuring that characters reference the gap period naturally and consistently.

## The plot arc section

### How do you track multiple arcs across a series?

A well-planned series runs multiple arcs simultaneously: the series-level overarching arc, each book's individual arc, and several subplot arcs that weave through multiple books. Tracking these prevents dropped threads — setups that never pay off, questions that are never answered, and promises that are never kept.

Create an arc tracker with three tiers. The series arc: one entry describing the overarching question, its key milestones across books, and its planned resolution. Book arcs: one entry per book describing the central conflict, its resolution, and how it advances the series arc. Subplot arcs: one entry per subplot describing its introduction point, its development across books, and its planned resolution.

For each subplot, explicitly note: which book introduced it, which books advance it, and which book resolves it. A subplot introduced in book one that is not mentioned in books two or three and then suddenly resolved in book four feels disconnected. Plan regular touches — brief references, small developments, escalating tension — that keep the subplot alive in the reader's awareness.

### What is a setup-and-payoff tracker?

Every time you plant a detail that requires future payoff — a mysterious object, an unexplained character behavior, a prophecy, a secret — add it to a setup-and-payoff tracker. Columns: the setup (what you planted), the book and chapter where it was planted, the planned payoff (what it leads to), and the book where the payoff occurs.

This tracker is your promise ledger. Readers remember setups even when authors forget them. An unexplained mystery introduced in book one that is never resolved is a broken promise that can generate one-star reviews on the series finale. The tracker ensures every promise is kept.

## Maintaining the bible across the series

### When do you update the series bible?

Update the bible after completing each book, before beginning the next one. Read through the completed manuscript with the bible open and add every new character detail, world-building fact, timeline event, and plot development. This post-book update is the most important maintenance task — it captures everything while the details are fresh.

Additionally, consult the bible at the start of each writing session when you are working on book two or later. Before writing a scene, check: the characters' current physical state, their knowledge state, their relationship status, and any relevant world-building details. This takes five minutes and prevents hours of retroactive correction.

### How detailed is too detailed?

A series bible should contain every detail that could cause a continuity error if forgotten. It should not contain every sentence of prose or every minor worldbuilding thought. The test: if you would need to search through your manuscripts to verify this detail while writing a future book, it belongs in the bible. If it is obvious or self-evident from context, it does not.

Most working series bibles for a five-book series are 20-40 pages — substantial but manageable. Longer bibles risk becoming unusable. If your bible is growing beyond this range, consider splitting it into separate documents by category (characters, world, timeline) with a master index for cross-reference.
