# Dopamine Menu for ADHD: A Step-by-Step Guide to Building Your Personal Dopamenu in 2026

If you have ADHD, you already know the feeling. You finish one task and then stare at the wall, unable to start the next one. You are not tired exactly, and you are not lazy. Your brain is simply running on empty in the one currency it cares about most — stimulation. That gap between knowing what you should do and actually doing it has a name in the ADHD community, and it also has a surprisingly practical solution. It is called the dopamine menu, sometimes written as "dopamenu," and once you understand how it works, it can change the way you move through your entire day.

## What exactly is a dopamine menu?

A dopamine menu is a personalized, pre-made list of activities that reliably give your brain the stimulation it needs, organized into categories the same way a restaurant menu is organized into starters, mains, sides, and desserts. The concept was popularized by ADHD clinician and podcaster Eric Tivers and gained mainstream traction through the YouTube channel How to ADHD, hosted by Jessica McCabe. Since then it has become one of the most shared coping tools in the ADHD community, appearing on TikTok, Reddit, therapy worksheets, and even Etsy printables.

The key idea is deceptively simple. You build the menu when your brain is regulated and clear. You use the menu when your brain is depleted and foggy. By separating the moment of deciding from the moment of choosing, you remove the biggest obstacle ADHD throws in front of you — decision fatigue at the exact moment you can least afford it.

### Why does a dopamine menu work specifically for ADHD brains?

ADHD involves under-active dopamine signaling in the brain's reward and motivation circuits. This does not mean people with ADHD lack dopamine entirely. It means the system that connects effort to reward fires less reliably than it does in neurotypical brains. When stimulation drops below a certain threshold, motivation collapses, focus scatters, and the brain starts hunting for the nearest hit of engagement, which is usually a phone screen.

The dopamine menu works because it intercepts that hunt before it turns into a two-hour scroll session. Instead of asking your depleted brain to generate an idea from scratch — a task that requires the exact executive function ADHD impairs — you hand it a short list of pre-approved options. The cognitive load drops from "think of something to do" to "pick one of these." That difference sounds small, but for an ADHD brain caught in an understimulation spiral, it can be the difference between a lost afternoon and a productive reset.

There is also a reframing benefit that matters more than most guides acknowledge. The menu treats restlessness and stimulation-seeking as a legitimate neurological need rather than a character flaw. You are not procrastinating because you are broken. You are understimulated because your brain works differently, and the menu is how you meet that need on your own terms.

## The four categories of a dopamine menu

### What goes into each section of the dopamenu?

The restaurant metaphor is not just cute — it is functional. Each category corresponds to a different level of time, energy, and consequence, so you can match your current situation to the right kind of stimulation.

**Starters** are quick hits that take under five minutes and require no setup. These are the activities you reach for when you need an immediate reset between tasks or when you feel yourself glazing over in a meeting. Playing a loud, energizing song at full volume counts. So does stepping outside for sixty seconds of fresh air, splashing cold water on your face, doing ten pushups, stretching, petting your dog, or chewing a piece of gum. The goal is not to entertain yourself. The goal is to nudge your dopamine signaling just enough to re-engage with whatever you were doing before.

**Mains** are substantial activities that take thirty minutes or more and provide deep engagement. These are the anchors of your day outside of work obligations. A gym session, a long walk, cooking a new recipe, working on a craft project, playing a musical instrument, meeting a friend for coffee, or diving into a hobby that genuinely absorbs you. Mains require more activation energy to start, which is exactly why they need to be written down in advance. When your brain is depleted, it will never spontaneously suggest going for a run. But if "run the river loop" is already on the menu, you only have to say yes instead of inventing the idea from nothing.

**Sides** are not standalone activities. They are things that make boring but necessary tasks bearable enough to get through. A curated playlist that makes data entry tolerable. A podcast that turns dishwashing into something you almost look forward to. Body doubling, whether in person or through an online coworking stream, that gives you just enough social accountability to stay seated. A standing desk that adds physical movement to sedentary work. Flavored sparkling water that makes a long writing session feel slightly more like a treat. Sides are the secret weapon of the dopamine menu because they do not compete with your responsibilities — they attach themselves to your responsibilities and make them possible.

**Desserts** are the high-stimulation, high-cost options. Video games, social media scrolling, online shopping, binge-watching a series, or any activity that delivers a massive dopamine hit but tends to consume more time, money, or energy than you intended. The critical insight here is that desserts should be portioned, not banned. If you remove them entirely from your menu, you will abandon the menu within a week because it will feel like a punishment rather than a tool. Instead, treat them the way you would treat actual dessert — enjoy them deliberately, in a defined window, after you have had your mains and starters. Setting a timer for thirty minutes of gaming after completing a work block is sustainable. Telling yourself you will never game again is not.

## How to build your own dopamine menu step by step

### How do you figure out which activities belong on your menu?

The biggest mistake people make with their first dopamine menu is filling it with aspirational activities they think they should enjoy rather than activities they actually do enjoy. A menu that says "meditate for twenty minutes" when you have never successfully meditated in your life is not a tool — it is a guilt list. Start with reality instead.

For one week, keep a simple log of what you actually do when you are understimulated. Note what pulls you in without effort and what genuinely resets your focus afterward. Pay attention to the difference between activities that leave you feeling better and activities that feel good in the moment but leave you worse off. This audit is the raw material for your menu, and it has to be honest to be useful.

Once you have your list, sort each item into one of the four categories based on two factors — how much time it requires and how much it costs you in terms of energy, money, or subsequent productivity. An activity that takes five minutes and leaves you ready to work is a starter. An activity that takes two hours and leaves you unable to do anything else is a dessert. Most of the sorting is intuitive once you start.

### Where should you put your dopamine menu so you actually use it?

Visibility is everything. A dopamine menu saved in a notes app you never open is functionally identical to not having one. The menu needs to be in your face at the exact moments you need it, which means placing it where your eyes land during transition points — the moments between tasks when understimulation typically strikes.

The refrigerator door works if your kitchen is a common pacing destination. A sticky note on your monitor works if you tend to zone out at your desk. Your phone's lock screen works if you usually reach for your phone first when boredom hits. Some people use a laminated card they keep next to their keyboard. Others pin it to a cork board above their desk. The format matters far less than the placement. If you will not see it without looking for it, you will not use it.

### How do you keep the menu working over time?

Stimulation fades with familiarity. This is true for everyone, but it is especially true for ADHD brains, which habituate to reward faster than neurotypical brains do. An activity that was a reliable starter in January may feel completely flat by April. This is not a failure of the menu — it is a feature of your neurology, and the fix is straightforward.

Review and refresh your menu every two to three months. Drop items that no longer deliver any reset. Add new ones you have discovered since the last revision. Pay attention to seasonal shifts, because outdoor activities that work in summer need indoor replacements in winter. The menu is a living document, not a permanent prescription.

Another powerful technique is attaching specific menu items to predictable trigger points in your day. If you always hit a wall at three in the afternoon, pre-assign a starter for that exact moment. If the transition from work to evening routines is where you lose two hours to scrolling, assign a main for that window. The more automatic the connection between trigger and response becomes, the less executive function you need to deploy the menu, which is the entire point.

## The science behind the dopamine menu

### Is there actual research supporting dopamine menus?

Honesty matters here. The term "dopamine menu" is pop-neuroscience shorthand, not a clinical intervention with randomized controlled trials behind it. No study has tested the dopamine menu as a standalone treatment for ADHD. Activities do not release dopamine in neat, measurable units the way the metaphor implies.

That said, the underlying mechanics are well supported by existing research. The concept of implementation intentions — deciding in advance what you will do in a specific situation — has decades of evidence showing it reduces the gap between intention and action. Pre-commitment strategies are a cornerstone of cognitive behavioral approaches to ADHD. The idea of tiered coping lists appears in dialectical behavior therapy under different names. And the neuroscience of ADHD's reward signaling dysfunction is established enough that it forms the basis for how stimulant medications work.

So while you will not find a peer-reviewed paper titled "Efficacy of Dopamine Menus in Adults with ADHD," you will find substantial support for every individual piece the menu is built from. The ADHD community has essentially assembled a practical coping structure from sound clinical building blocks, and clinicians increasingly recommend it as a complement to formal treatment.

### When should you seek professional help beyond a dopamine menu?

A dopamine menu is a coping tool, not a replacement for treatment. If understimulation regularly disrupts your work, your studies, your finances, or your relationships — if you are consistently unable to start tasks despite knowing exactly what needs to happen — the menu alone is not enough. ADHD-adapted cognitive behavioral therapy, coaching, and medication are the evidence-based interventions that address the neurological roots of the problem, and a dopamine menu works best as one piece of a broader strategy that includes professional guidance.

If your stimulation-seeking has shifted toward patterns that carry real consequences — compulsive spending, substance use, risky impulsive decisions — that is a signal to talk to a clinician sooner rather than later. The menu can help you manage everyday understimulation, but it was never designed to treat the full spectrum of ADHD challenges on its own.

## Start building your dopamine menu today

Understanding your ADHD brain is the first step toward working with it instead of against it. A dopamine menu will not cure your ADHD, but it will give you a concrete, actionable tool for the moments when your motivation disappears and your brain demands stimulation. Build it honestly, keep it visible, refresh it often, and treat it as what it is — a user manual for a brain that runs on a different operating system.
