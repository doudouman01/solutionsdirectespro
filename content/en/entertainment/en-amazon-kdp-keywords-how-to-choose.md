# Amazon KDP Keywords: How to Choose the Right 7 for Your Book

When you publish on Amazon KDP, you get seven keyword fields. Each field accepts a phrase of up to 50 characters. These 350 total characters — fewer than two tweets — are one of the most consequential inputs in your entire publishing business. They determine which searches surface your book, which categories Amazon assigns you to, and how the recommendation algorithm classifies your book for "customers also bought" suggestions.

And most authors fill them out in three minutes, using whatever genre terms come to mind first, and never touch them again.

This is the equivalent of opening a store on the busiest street in the world and putting up a blank sign. Your book might be extraordinary. But if your keywords do not match what readers are actually searching for, those readers will never see it. They will find the book next to yours — the one whose author spent an hour on keyword research instead of three minutes.

Here is how to be that author.

## Understanding how Amazon keywords work

### What do keywords actually do on Amazon?

Keywords serve three functions on Amazon. First, they determine search visibility — when a reader types a query into Amazon's search bar, your book appears in the results only if your keywords (along with your title, subtitle, and description) match or closely relate to that query. No match, no visibility, regardless of how perfect your book is for that reader.

Second, keywords influence category placement. Amazon uses your keywords (in combination with the categories you select during upload) to place your book in browse categories. Certain keyword phrases trigger placement in specific categories that are not available through the standard category selection dropdown. This is how authors access niche categories with less competition and higher ranking potential.

Third, keywords feed the recommendation algorithm. Amazon's "customers who bought this also bought" and "recommended for you" features rely partly on keyword similarity between books. Books with similar keyword profiles are more likely to be recommended alongside each other — which means your keywords determine not just who finds you through search, but who finds you through algorithmic recommendation.

### What makes a good keyword phrase?

A good keyword phrase has three qualities: relevance (it accurately describes your book), search volume (real readers actually type this phrase into Amazon), and manageable competition (the number of books targeting this phrase is small enough that yours can rank).

The ideal keyword is specific enough to attract readers who will enjoy your book and broad enough to have meaningful search traffic. "Romance" is too broad — millions of results, impossible to rank. "Enemies to lovers forced proximity small town romance" is more specific — fewer results, higher relevance, and the readers who search this phrase know exactly what they want. If your book delivers it, you have a highly qualified buyer.

Each of your seven fields should target a different search query. Duplicating the same concept across multiple fields wastes slots. "Romance enemies to lovers" and "enemies to lovers romance novel" are essentially the same search — use one field for this concept and use the other six for different angles.

## The research process

### How do you find keywords readers actually search for?

Amazon's search bar is your primary research tool — and it is free. Start typing a genre term and watch the autocomplete suggestions. These suggestions are generated from real reader searches, ranked by frequency. They represent actual demand, not guesswork.

For a contemporary romance, type "contemporary romance" and note every autocomplete suggestion: "contemporary romance books," "contemporary romance enemies to lovers," "contemporary romance small town," "contemporary romance kindle unlimited." Each suggestion is a validated search query with proven reader demand.

Go deeper. Type "enemies to lovers" and note the autocomplete: "enemies to lovers romance," "enemies to lovers dark romance," "enemies to lovers fantasy," "enemies to lovers bully romance." Each of these represents a distinct reader search with distinct intent. If your book fits one of these specific queries, you have found a keyword candidate.

Repeat this process with every relevant term: your subgenre, your tropes (for fiction), your topic areas (for nonfiction), your setting, your character types, and your comparable author names (Amazon allows competitor author names as keywords, though this is a gray area some authors choose to avoid).

### How do you evaluate keyword competition?

Search each candidate keyword phrase on Amazon and examine the results. Three indicators tell you whether the competition is manageable.

First, the number of results. A search returning 2,000-20,000 results is in the competitive sweet spot for most indie authors — enough demand to indicate a real market, not so much competition that your book drowns. Searches with 50,000+ results are extremely competitive and should only be targeted if your book has strong reviews, an established author name, or advertising support.

Second, the quality of competing books on the first page. Are the top results from major publishers with thousands of reviews, or from indie authors with modest review counts? If the first page shows indie books with 50-200 reviews, a well-produced new release can realistically compete. If it shows traditionally published bestsellers with 10,000+ reviews, your book will not reach page one without significant advertising spend.

Third, the relevance of results. If your search returns books that do not match the query well — generic results that are clearly not targeting this specific keyword — it means the keyword space is underserved. This is an opportunity: your book, properly optimized for this keyword, can capture traffic that existing books are poorly serving.

### Should you use keyword research tools?

Publisher Rocket is the most widely used keyword research tool for KDP authors. It provides estimated monthly search volumes, competition scores, and suggested keywords that Amazon's autocomplete does not reveal. At $97 one-time, it pays for itself if it helps you find even two or three high-value keywords you would have missed.

The limitation of all keyword tools is that Amazon does not share actual search volume data publicly. Every tool is estimating based on indirect signals. Use tool data as a directional guide, not as precise measurement. Cross-reference tool suggestions with your own Amazon autocomplete research and competitor analysis.

Free alternatives: Amazon autocomplete (as described above), Google Keyword Planner (search trends correlate loosely with Amazon search behavior), and manual competitor analysis (what keywords are successful comparable books clearly targeting?).

## The 7-field strategy

### How should you allocate your seven keyword fields?

Each field should serve a distinct strategic purpose. A proven allocation framework uses seven different keyword angles:

Field one: your primary subgenre phrase. The most specific genre descriptor that matches your book. "Dark enemies to lovers romance" or "cozy small town mystery" or "dystopian science fiction series." This is your broadest genre signal.

Field two: your dominant trope or topic. The primary story pattern (fiction) or subject area (nonfiction) that readers search for. "Fake dating romantic comedy" or "personal finance for millennials."

Field three: a secondary trope or topic. A different searchable element of your book. "Slow burn forbidden love" or "investing for beginners."

Field four: a setting or context descriptor. Where and when your story takes place, or the context of your nonfiction advice. "Small town southern romance" or "work from home productivity."

Field five: a comparable author or "readers who like" phrase. A phrase that positions your book alongside established authors your target readers already enjoy. "Books like Colleen Hoover" or "similar to atomic habits."

Field six: an emotional or experience descriptor. The feeling or outcome your book delivers. "Heartwarming feel good romance" or "practical money saving tips."

Field seven: a category trigger keyword. A specific phrase designed to unlock a browse category not available through standard selection. These phrases vary by genre and change periodically — research current trigger phrases for your specific categories.

### What should you never put in a keyword field?

Never waste a field on your book title, author name, or genre words that already appear in your title or subtitle — Amazon already indexes those. Repeating them in keywords provides zero additional visibility.

Never use single generic words: "romance," "thriller," "self-help." The competition for these terms is astronomical, and a reader searching a single word is rarely ready to buy — they are browsing, not shopping. Specific phrases convert. Generic words waste fields.

Never include misleading keywords that do not accurately describe your book. Even if a keyword drives traffic, readers who arrive expecting one thing and find another will leave without buying — or worse, will buy and leave negative reviews about misrepresentation.

Never use formatting that Amazon does not process: quotation marks, excessive punctuation, or competitor ASINs. Keep keywords as clean, natural-language phrases.

## Optimization and maintenance

### How do you know if your keywords are working?

Amazon does not provide direct keyword performance data for organic search. But Amazon Ads provides indirect insights that are extremely valuable. When you run a Sponsored Products campaign with automatic targeting, Amazon shows you which search terms triggered impressions and clicks for your ad. These search terms reveal what readers are actually typing — and whether your keywords are aligning with real search behavior.

If a keyword you selected as a metadata keyword is also generating paid ad impressions with a strong click-through rate, you have confirmed that the keyword has search volume and relevance. If a keyword generates zero impressions even as an ad target, it may have too little search volume to be worth a metadata slot.

### How often should you update your keywords?

Review your keywords every 90 days or whenever you notice a significant change in organic sales. Keyword effectiveness shifts as competitor books enter and leave the market, as reader search behavior evolves, and as Amazon adjusts its algorithm.

The update process: run Amazon Ads in automatic mode for two weeks to collect fresh search term data. Compare your current keyword performance against the search terms that are actually driving impressions and clicks. Replace underperforming keywords with new phrases that your ad data suggests are in demand.

Seasonal adjustments also matter. A book about holiday entertaining will have different optimal keywords in November than in June. A book about tax planning peaks in search volume in January through April. Align your keyword strategy with the seasonal patterns of your topic.
