## The electromagnetic ocean we swim in

Right now, as you read these words, your body is being penetrated by thousands of invisible signals. Wi-Fi routers, cell towers, Bluetooth devices, GPS satellites, radio stations — the air around you is thick with electromagnetic radiation, all operating at frequencies your senses cannot detect.

We've collectively decided this is fine. And for the most part, it is. The frequencies used in consumer electronics operate at power levels far too low to cause biological harm — at least, according to our current understanding.

But science fiction has always been at its most powerful when it takes a current reality and asks: what if we're wrong?

## The frontier of neurotechnology

The idea of signals affecting the brain isn't pure fantasy. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) already uses electromagnetic pulses to treat depression by stimulating specific brain regions. Brain-computer interfaces are advancing rapidly, with companies developing implants that translate neural signals into digital commands.

The gap between "stimulating a specific brain region for therapeutic purposes" and "broadcasting a signal that alters cognition at scale" is enormous — but it's a gap measured in engineering challenges, not physical impossibility. The principles are sound. The question is whether the technology could be scaled, weaponized, and deployed without detection.

This is precisely the territory that near-future sci-fi thrillers like *Dead Frequency* explore. Not the impossible, but the improbable-but-plausible. The kind of scenario that makes you look at your devices a little differently after you finish the last page.

## When fiction becomes a warning

The best science fiction doesn't predict the future — it illuminates the present. George Orwell didn't predict surveillance states; he warned about the totalitarian impulse that already existed in his time.

In the same tradition, *Dead Frequency* by Adrian Phoenix Vale uses the near-future setting not as escapism, but as a lens. The signal in the story is fictional. But the questions it raises — about the invisible infrastructure we've built around ourselves, about the trust we place in systems we don't understand, about the line between connectivity and vulnerability — are urgently real.

In a world where algorithms already shape our opinions, curate our information, and influence our decisions in ways we barely comprehend, the question isn't whether invisible signals can control your mind. The question is: how would you know if they already were?
