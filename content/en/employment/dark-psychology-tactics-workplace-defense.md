# 5 Dark Psychology Tactics Used in the Workplace (And How to Defend Yourself)

You've had the experience. The colleague who takes credit for your work in front of the boss. The manager who changes expectations after the fact. The coworker who is charming to your face and devastating behind your back.

You can't quite name what's happening — but you know something is off. You feel drained, frustrated, and somehow always on the defensive.

**What you're experiencing has a name: workplace manipulation.** And it's far more common than most people realize.

A 2023 study published in the *Journal of Business Ethics* found that approximately 1 in 5 corporate leaders exhibits significant traits of the Dark Triad — narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy. These traits don't prevent career success; in many competitive environments, they accelerate it.

Understanding these tactics isn't about becoming paranoid. It's about developing the professional equivalent of self-defense: the ability to recognize manipulation in real time and respond in ways that protect your career, your reputation, and your sanity.

## Tactic 1: Credit Hijacking

### How It Works

You present an idea in a meeting. Your colleague paraphrases it two minutes later — slightly reframed — and receives the credit. Or worse: you share a concept privately, and weeks later it appears in someone else's presentation with no attribution.

Credit hijacking is effective because it's hard to prove and easy to dismiss. "Great minds think alike" becomes the cover story. Pointing it out makes you look petty — which is exactly what the hijacker is counting on.

### The Strategic Defense

**Before sharing ideas:** Send an email summarizing your concept to relevant stakeholders — with a timestamp. "Following up on the approach I outlined, here's a summary." Now there's a paper trail.

**In the moment:** When someone paraphrases your idea, don't accuse — reclaim. "I'm glad that resonated — building on the approach I outlined earlier, here's the next step." Calm, factual, devastating.

**Long-term:** Build a documentation habit. Weekly emails to your manager summarizing your contributions and ideas are not "bragging" — they're professional practice that makes credit hijacking much harder.

## Tactic 2: Strategic Ambiguity

### How It Works

The manager who gives you vague instructions, then criticizes you for not meeting expectations that were never clearly stated. The colleague who agrees to a plan verbally, then denies the agreement later: "That's not what I understood."

Strategic ambiguity creates a no-win situation: you can't succeed because success was never defined, and you can't defend yourself because there's no record of what was actually agreed.

### The Strategic Defense

**Follow up everything in writing.** After verbal conversations, send a brief email: "To confirm our discussion, here's my understanding of next steps: [X, Y, Z]. Let me know if I'm missing anything."

If the other person doesn't correct your summary, you have documented agreement. If they do correct it, you have clarity. Either way, you win.

**When criticized for ambiguous expectations:** "I'd like to understand your expectations more clearly so I can deliver exactly what you need. Can we document the specific criteria for this project?"

This response is unassailable — it frames your request as a commitment to quality, not a defense against manipulation.

## Tactic 3: Triangulation

### How It Works

A colleague tells you that another colleague said something negative about you. Or a manager shares "concerns" that unnamed team members have raised about your performance. Or someone creates conflict between you and a coworker by selectively sharing information with each of you.

Triangulation serves multiple purposes: it creates distrust between potential allies, positions the manipulator as the information broker (a powerful role), and keeps everyone off-balance while the manipulator appears neutral.

### The Strategic Defense

**Verify directly.** When someone tells you "Person X said this about you," go to Person X directly — calmly. "I heard something that didn't sound right, and I wanted to check with you directly." In most cases, the message was distorted or fabricated entirely.

**Don't play the game.** When someone tries to draw you into speaking negatively about a colleague, redirect: "I'd rather discuss that with them directly." This one sentence shuts down most triangulation attempts.

**Watch for patterns.** If the same person is consistently the source of interpersonal conflicts that they're never directly involved in, that's a signal. Document the pattern — dates, claims, what you verified.

## Tactic 4: Gaslighting at Work

### How It Works

You clearly remember a conversation, a decision, an agreement — and your manager or colleague flatly denies it happened. "I never said that." "That's not what we agreed on." "You're misremembering."

Professional gaslighting is especially effective because workplace power dynamics make it harder to push back. When your boss says "I never approved that," contradicting them feels risky — even when you know you're right.

Over time, repeated gaslighting erodes your confidence in your own judgment. You start second-guessing yourself, documenting obsessively (which the gaslighter then frames as "paranoid"), and losing the assertiveness that made you effective.

### The Strategic Defense

**Documentation is your shield.** Email confirmations after every significant conversation. Meeting notes shared with attendees. Calendar invites with agendas. These aren't excessive — they're standard professional practice that happens to make gaslighting nearly impossible.

**Trust your first reaction.** Your initial experience of a situation — before someone "corrects" it — is almost always more accurate than the revised version. If something feels wrong, it probably is.

**Talk to peers you trust.** Gaslighting works through isolation. When you share your experience with trusted colleagues and they validate it ("Yes, I remember that meeting the same way you do"), it breaks the spell.

## Tactic 5: Weaponized Emotions

### How It Works

The colleague who cries when confronted about missed deadlines — shifting the conversation from accountability to comfort. The boss who explodes with anger disproportionate to the situation — training you to avoid bringing up problems. The coworker who sulks or withdraws when they don't get their way — creating an emotional penalty for disagreement.

Weaponized emotions use your empathy, your desire for harmony, and your conflict avoidance against you. The result: you stop holding people accountable, stop raising legitimate concerns, and start managing their emotions instead of doing your job.

### The Strategic Defense

**Separate the emotion from the issue.** "I can see you're upset, and I want to give you space for that. When you're ready, I still need us to address [the actual issue]."

This response is compassionate without being manipulated. It acknowledges the emotion without letting it derail the conversation.

**Don't absorb guilt for someone else's reaction.** If your legitimate, reasonable behavior causes someone a disproportionate emotional response, that's information about them — not feedback about you.

**Maintain consistency.** The key to defending against weaponized emotions is responding the same way every time. When the manipulator learns that crying, anger, or withdrawal no longer changes the outcome, they lose the incentive to use those tactics with you.

## The Meta-Defense: Why Composure Is Your Greatest Asset

Every tactic on this list has one thing in common: **it only works if you react emotionally.** Credit hijacking works when you get visibly frustrated. Gaslighting works when you get flustered and defensive. Triangulation works when you get angry at the third party instead of the source.

The single most powerful professional defense is the ability to remain composed when provoked. Not cold — composed. Not indifferent — deliberate.

When you stay calm while someone is trying to destabilize you, three things happen:
1. **You think more clearly** — your prefrontal cortex stays engaged
2. **They lose their advantage** — their tactic depends on your reaction
3. **Others notice** — the composed person in a conflict always looks more credible

This isn't a personality trait — it's a trainable skill. And it's the foundation of every effective response to workplace manipulation.
