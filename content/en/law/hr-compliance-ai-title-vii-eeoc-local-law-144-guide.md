# HR Compliance and AI in 2026: Title VII, EEOC Guidance, Local Law 144, and Every Framework You Need to Know

Using AI in human resources without understanding the legal landscape is the fastest way to generate a lawsuit in 2026.

That's not an exaggeration. The EEOC has made AI enforcement a strategic priority. Class-action firms are actively recruiting plaintiffs who were evaluated by biased algorithms. And the patchwork of federal, state, and local regulations governing AI in employment decisions is expanding every quarter.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: most HR teams using AI right now are out of compliance. Not because they're careless, but because the regulatory landscape evolved faster than their implementation practices.

This guide covers every framework that matters — what it requires, how it applies to AI, and what you need to do about it.

## Federal Frameworks

### Title VII of the Civil Rights Act

Title VII prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, and national origin. It applies to AI in a critical way: **disparate impact**.

You don't need to intend discrimination. If your AI tool produces outcomes that disproportionately disadvantage a protected class — fewer interview invitations for women, lower scores for candidates with traditionally Black names, reduced hiring rates for older workers — you're in violation.

**What this means for AI in HR:**

- Resume screening tools must be tested for adverse impact across all protected categories
- Job description generators must be audited for language that discourages protected groups from applying
- Interview evaluation AI must be validated to ensure it doesn't penalize speech patterns, accents, or communication styles associated with protected classes
- Performance review AI must not produce systematically different outcomes based on demographic characteristics

**The four-fifths rule still applies.** If the selection rate for a protected group is less than four-fifths (80%) of the rate for the group with the highest selection rate, disparate impact is presumed. This applies whether a human or an algorithm made the selection.

### Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)

The ADA requires reasonable accommodation — and AI systems often fail here without explicit safeguards.

**Common ADA violations with AI:**

- Video interview AI that penalizes candidates with speech impediments, hearing aids, facial differences, or mobility limitations
- Cognitive assessment AI that doesn't accommodate learning disabilities
- Chatbot-based application processes that aren't accessible to screen readers
- Timed assessments that don't allow extended time for qualified candidates

**The fix:** Every AI tool used in the application or evaluation process must include an accommodation pathway. Candidates must be able to request (and receive) alternative evaluation methods. And HR must document that the accommodation was provided.

### Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)

AI-powered recruitment tools are particularly susceptible to age discrimination — often unintentionally. If your AI favors candidates with recent graduation dates, familiarity with specific modern technologies, or "cultural fit" indicators that correlate with age, you're exposed.

**Watch for:** AI tools that filter based on years of experience (maximum caps), graduation recency, social media presence intensity, or technology stack familiarity that isn't a bona fide job requirement.

### Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA)

AI-powered attendance tracking and performance monitoring systems must account for FMLA-protected leave. If your AI flags employees with intermittent FMLA leave as attendance problems or productivity underperformers, you're in violation.

### EEOC AI Guidance

The EEOC has issued detailed guidance on AI in employment decisions. The core principles:

**Employer responsibility is non-delegable.** You are responsible for the AI tools you use — even if a vendor built them, trained them, and operates them. "The vendor told us it was compliant" is not a defense.

**Selection procedure analysis applies.** AI-powered hiring tools are "selection procedures" under the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures. All testing and validation requirements apply.

**Reasonable accommodation extends to AI.** Employers must provide alternatives when an AI-powered assessment creates barriers for individuals with disabilities.

**Transparency matters.** The EEOC expects employers to be able to explain how their AI tools work and what factors they consider. "It's a black box" is a red flag, not an explanation.

## State and Local Frameworks

### NYC Local Law 144 (Automated Employment Decision Tools)

The most significant local AI employment regulation in the United States. If you use any "automated employment decision tool" (AEDT) to screen candidates or employees in New York City:

**You must:**
1. Conduct an **independent bias audit** annually — testing for disparate impact across sex/gender and race/ethnicity categories
2. **Publish** the audit results on your careers page
3. Provide **10 business days' notice** to candidates before using an AEDT
4. Disclose the **job qualifications and characteristics** the AEDT evaluates
5. Provide candidates with information about the **data sources** used
6. Allow candidates to **request an alternative** selection process

**Penalties:** Up to $1,500 per violation. With 250 applicants per posting, a single non-compliant job opening could result in $375,000 in fines.

### Illinois Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act (AIPA)

If you use AI to analyze video interviews of candidates in Illinois:

- **Written notice** must be provided before the interview explaining that AI will analyze the video
- The notice must explain **how the AI works** and what characteristics it evaluates
- **Written consent** from the candidate is required
- The employer must have a **publicly available policy** on data destruction
- Video recordings must be **destroyed** within 30 days of a candidate's request
- Sharing videos with third parties is **restricted**

### Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205)

Colorado's law takes a broader approach, covering all "high-risk AI systems" — which explicitly includes AI used in employment decisions.

**Requirements for deployers (employers):**
- Implement a **risk management policy** for AI systems
- Complete an **impact assessment** before deploying high-risk AI
- Notify consumers (candidates/employees) when AI is used in consequential decisions
- Provide an **opportunity to correct** data the AI relied on
- Provide an **opportunity to appeal** AI-influenced decisions with human review

### CCPA/CPRA (California)

California's privacy framework impacts AI in HR by giving candidates and employees rights over their personal data:

- **Right to know** what personal information is collected and how it's used by AI systems
- **Right to delete** personal information from AI training datasets
- **Right to opt out** of automated decision-making in certain contexts
- **Right to non-discrimination** for exercising privacy rights

### OFCCP Requirements

Federal contractors face additional scrutiny. The Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs (OFCCP) has signaled increased focus on AI in employment decisions:

- AI tools must be validated under **Uniform Guidelines** standards
- **Adverse impact analyses** must include AI-powered processes
- **Record retention** requirements extend to AI tool inputs, outputs, and decision rationale
- Affirmative action plans must address AI's role in selection processes

## Building a Compliance-First AI Implementation

### Step 1: Inventory your AI tools

Document every AI tool touching any employment decision — hiring, performance, compensation, promotion, termination. Include vendor-provided tools, internal tools, and even individual managers using ChatGPT to draft performance reviews.

### Step 2: Map regulatory exposure

For each tool, identify which regulations apply based on:
- Geographic location of candidates and employees
- Type of employment decision the tool influences
- Whether the company is a federal contractor
- Industry-specific regulations

### Step 3: Conduct bias audits

Test every AI tool for disparate impact across all protected categories. Document the results. If adverse impact is found, either modify the tool or document a business necessity justification — and ensure no less-discriminatory alternative exists.

### Step 4: Build notice and consent processes

Create standardized disclosure language for candidates and employees. Build consent mechanisms into your application and evaluation processes. Ensure accommodation request pathways exist and are communicated.

### Step 5: Establish governance

Assign ownership for AI compliance. This isn't a one-time project — it's an ongoing responsibility. Someone in your organization must be accountable for:
- Monitoring regulatory changes
- Conducting regular audits
- Responding to candidate and employee complaints
- Updating tools and processes as requirements evolve

### Step 6: Document everything

If it isn't documented, it didn't happen. Maintain records of:
- AI tool descriptions and vendor contracts
- Bias audit results and remediation actions
- Notice and consent records
- Accommodation requests and responses
- Human review documentation for AI-influenced decisions
- Training records for employees using AI tools

## What's Coming Next

The regulatory landscape is expanding, not contracting. Expect:

- **Federal AI legislation** addressing employment decisions specifically
- **More states** adopting NYC Local Law 144-style requirements
- **Increased EEOC enforcement** with dedicated AI investigation teams
- **EU AI Act influence** on multinational employers operating in the United States
- **Industry-specific regulations** for AI in healthcare, financial services, and education employment
