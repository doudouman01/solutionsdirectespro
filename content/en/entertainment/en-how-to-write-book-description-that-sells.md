# How to Write a Book Description That Sells: The Indie Author's Guide

There is a brutal irony in self-publishing. An author will spend six months writing a novel, weeks editing it, hundreds of dollars on a professional cover — and then write their book description in fifteen minutes, copy-pasting a plot summary that reads like a book report from a bored eighth-grader.

That fifteen-minute description is costing them more sales than every other decision combined.

On Amazon, the book description is the final conversion point. The cover earns the click. The title and reviews build interest. But the description is where the reader decides: buy or back. And the data is unforgiving. A well-crafted description converts 10-15% of page visitors into buyers. A generic summary converts 1-2%. For a book getting 1,000 page views per month, that is the difference between 100 sales and 15 sales — from the exact same traffic, the exact same book, the exact same cover.

Your description is not a summary. It is a sales page. And writing it is a skill that can be learned.

## The psychology of the book description

### Why do readers read descriptions?

They are not reading for information. They already know the genre (the cover told them), the rough subject (the title told them), and the quality level (the reviews told them). By the time they reach the description, they are looking for one thing: emotional permission to buy.

They want to feel the book's energy. They want to sense the voice. They want to experience a flash of the emotion the book will deliver — excitement, intrigue, warmth, dread, humor, heartbreak — compressed into 150 words. If your description delivers that flash, the sale is made. If it reads like a Wikipedia entry, they scroll past.

This is why the best book descriptions do not describe the book. They sell the experience of reading the book. There is a fundamental difference, and understanding it changes everything about how you approach the task.

### How long should a book description be?

Amazon displays approximately 150-200 words before the "Read more" fold on desktop, and even less on mobile. Everything above the fold must do the heavy lifting — hook the reader, establish tone, create urgency. Below the fold, you have room for supporting details, but most purchase decisions are made before the reader clicks "Read more."

Total description length should be 150-300 words for fiction and 200-400 words for nonfiction. Shorter is almost always better. Every word that does not actively sell the book is a word that dilutes the ones that do. Cut ruthlessly.

## The anatomy of a converting description

### What makes a great opening hook?

The first line of your description is the most valuable real estate in your entire publishing business. It appears in search results, in "also bought" recommendations, and at the top of your product page. If it fails, nothing below it matters because nobody will read it.

Great opening hooks come in several forms. The provocative statement: a bold claim or observation that stops the scroll. The situational hook: drop the reader directly into the character's world at a moment of tension. The question hook: pose a question that the reader's curiosity will not let them walk away from. The stakes hook: name what can be won or lost in terms that resonate emotionally.

What does not work: character introductions ("Meet Sarah, a 32-year-old teacher who..."), setting descriptions ("In a small town in Vermont..."), or throat-clearing preamble ("In this gripping tale of..."). These openings signal amateur writing and generic storytelling — two things readers have been trained to scroll past.

### How should you structure the middle section?

After the hook, the middle of your description has one job: escalate. Every sentence should raise the emotional temperature by introducing complications, deepening the stakes, or revealing dimensions of the story that the hook only hinted at.

For fiction, this means layering conflict: the external obstacle, the internal wound, the relationship tension, the impossible choice. You are not telling the story — you are showing the reader the shape of the emotional rollercoaster they are about to board. Each sentence adds a loop, a drop, a twist to the ride.

For nonfiction, this means layering value: the problem you solve, the unique angle of your approach, the results readers can expect, and the credibility behind your advice. Nonfiction descriptions must answer the reader's fundamental question: "What will be different in my life after reading this?"

In both cases, specificity sells. "A thrilling adventure" means nothing. "A desperate midnight escape through the flooded tunnels beneath the city" means everything. Specific details create mental images. Mental images create emotional engagement. Emotional engagement creates sales.

### How do you write a closing that converts?

Your description should end with forward momentum — a final line that makes not buying the book feel like a loss. For fiction, this is typically a question or a cliffhanger that creates an itch the reader can only scratch by reading the book. For nonfiction, it is a call to action that connects the book to the reader's immediate desires.

The worst way to end a description is to resolve it. If you answer every question, relieve every tension, and summarize the outcome, you have given the reader the emotional experience for free. They have no reason to buy the book because you already delivered the satisfaction. Leave them wanting. That wanting is the sale.

## Common mistakes that kill conversions

### Why does a plot summary fail as a description?

A plot summary tells the reader what happens. A selling description tells the reader what they will feel. These are fundamentally different communications, and the first one does not generate sales because knowing what happens in a book is not the same as experiencing it.

Consider the difference: "Sarah discovers that her husband has been lying about his past. She investigates and uncovers a secret that changes everything." That is a summary. It conveys information but creates no emotional pull. Now consider: "She trusted him with her life. She should not have. Because the man sleeping beside her every night has a name she has never heard — and a past that explains the locked drawer, the burner phone, and the three a.m. drives to nowhere." Same story. Completely different emotional impact. The second version makes you want to know more. The first version makes you feel like you already know enough.

### What should you never include in a book description?

Spoilers, obviously — but the definition of "spoiler" for descriptions is broader than most authors realize. Any plot development beyond the initial setup dilutes the curiosity that drives purchases. The reader should understand the premise, the stakes, and the tone. They should not understand the plot.

Also avoid: the word "journey" (the most overused word in self-publishing descriptions), rhetorical questions that are not genuinely provocative ("Will she find love?"), excessive adjectives ("an absolutely stunning, breathtakingly beautiful, heart-wrenchingly emotional tale"), and comparisons to famous authors or books unless they are genuinely earned and specific.

### Why do so many authors oversell?

Insecurity. Authors who are not confident in their book's ability to sell itself resort to hyperbole: "The most thrilling thriller you will ever read." "A romance that will change your life." "Readers are calling it the next Gone Girl." These claims trigger skepticism, not excitement. The reader's internal response is "prove it" — and a description that leads with claims rather than demonstration has already failed the proof.

Let your description demonstrate the book's quality through voice, specificity, and emotional resonance. Show, do not tell — the same principle that governs good fiction governs good descriptions.

## Genre-specific considerations

### How do fiction and nonfiction descriptions differ?

Fiction descriptions sell emotion. Nonfiction descriptions sell transformation. This fundamental difference drives every structural choice.

A fiction description should feel like the opening page of the book — same voice, same energy, same atmospheric quality. The reader is auditioning the writing style as much as the story concept. If your description is flat and your novel is vibrant, you are misrepresenting the product in the direction that loses sales.

A nonfiction description should feel like a conversation with a knowledgeable friend who has solved a problem you are struggling with. It should establish credibility (why should I trust this author?), name the pain point specifically (yes, that is exactly my problem), promise a concrete result (this is what will change), and preview the method (this is how we get there). Nonfiction readers are buying outcomes. The description must make those outcomes feel achievable and specific.

### Should you include keywords in your description?

Yes, but organically. Amazon's search algorithm does index book descriptions, which means strategically placed keywords can improve your discoverability. However, keyword-stuffed descriptions repel readers. The solution is to write naturally and then ensure that your key genre terms, tropes, and subject matter terms appear at least once.

For a contemporary romance, terms like "enemies to lovers," "small town," "second chance," or "slow burn" should appear naturally in the description if they describe the book. These are terms readers actively search for, and their presence signals genre fit to both the algorithm and the human reader.

## The revision process

### How do you test whether your description works?

Read it aloud. If it sounds like a book report, rewrite it. If it sounds like a conversation with a friend who is desperate to tell you about this amazing book they read — that is the target energy.

Show it to three people who read in your genre. Do not ask "Is this good?" Ask "Would you click Buy based on this?" The first question gets you politeness. The second gets you truth.

Compare your description to the top five bestsellers in your subcategory. Place yours side by side with theirs. If yours looks, sounds, and feels like it belongs in that company, you are ready. If it looks like it belongs on a different shelf — in quality, in energy, in voice — you have more work to do.
