# Self-Publishing Tools and Resources Every Indie Author Needs in 2026

Self-publishing in 2026 is a business with a tool for every task. The problem is not finding tools — it is choosing the right ones from the hundreds available, and avoiding the expensive mistakes of paying for premium services when free alternatives work just as well, or worse, using free alternatives when a paid tool would save you ten times its cost in time and results.

This guide maps the essential tools for every phase of the self-publishing workflow: writing, editing, covers, formatting, keywords and metadata, marketing, and operations. For each category, the recommendations are specific, current, and tested by working indie authors — not affiliate-driven lists designed to sell you the most expensive option.

## Writing tools

### What is the best software for writing a book?

The tool that lets you write consistently is the best tool. That said, three options dominate the indie author space for good reasons.

Scrivener remains the standard for long-form writing. Its organizational features — binder structure, research folders, corkboard view, split-screen editing — make it uniquely suited for managing complex novels and nonfiction projects. At $49 one-time, it is the best value in writing software. The learning curve is real but manageable, and the investment in learning it pays dividends on every project.

Google Docs works well for authors who want simplicity, cloud access, and easy collaboration with editors and beta readers. It lacks the organizational power of Scrivener but makes up for it with zero friction and universal accessibility.

Atticus is the newer option that combines writing and formatting in one tool. You can draft in Atticus and export directly to EPUB, MOBI, and print-ready PDF without switching applications. At $147 one-time, it is more expensive than Scrivener but eliminates the separate formatting step.

### How can AI tools help with writing?

AI has become a legitimate part of the indie author's toolkit — not as a replacement for writing, but as an accelerator for specific tasks. The most productive uses: brainstorming and outlining (generating plot structures, character profiles, and scene lists), research (quickly gathering factual information for nonfiction or historical fiction settings), first-draft acceleration (using AI to generate rough prose that the author then rewrites and polishes), and metadata creation (descriptions, keywords, taglines).

The critical principle: AI output must be transformed, not published. Readers, reviewers, and Amazon's own algorithms are increasingly sophisticated at detecting unedited AI prose. Authors who use AI as a brainstorming and drafting partner — then invest real craft in rewriting — produce better books faster. Authors who use AI as a substitute for writing produce mediocre books that damage their brand.

## Editing tools and services

### What editing does a self-published book need?

Every self-published book needs, at minimum, a proofread by someone other than the author. Ideally, a book receives three levels of editing: developmental editing (story structure, pacing, character arcs — $0.03-0.08 per word), copy editing (prose quality, consistency, grammar — $0.02-0.04 per word), and proofreading (final error catching — $0.01-0.02 per word).

Budget reality forces most indie authors to choose. If you can afford only one level, invest in a developmental edit for your first few books (to improve your craft) and a copy edit or proofread for every book thereafter (to catch errors your eye has learned to skip).

Where to find editors: Reedsy (a curated marketplace with vetted professionals), the Editorial Freelancers Association directory, genre-specific Facebook groups where authors share recommendations, and direct referrals from other indie authors in your genre.

### Are automated editing tools worth using?

ProWritingAid and Grammarly are useful as supplements but dangerous as substitutes for human editing. Both catch surface-level errors — grammar, spelling, passive voice, readability — that a careful self-edit might miss. ProWritingAid is generally preferred by fiction authors for its deeper style analysis, while Grammarly is faster for nonfiction and business writing.

Use these tools after your self-edit and before sending to a human editor. They catch the low-hanging errors, which lets your editor focus on higher-level issues. Never rely on them as your only editing step — they miss contextual errors, voice inconsistencies, plot holes, and the hundred other problems that only a human reader can identify.

## Cover design

### Where do you get professional covers?

Three paths, ranked by quality and cost. Premade covers ($50-200) are pre-designed covers sold exclusively to one author. They are designed by professionals who understand genre conventions, and they are available immediately. Browse premade cover marketplaces like The Book Cover Designer, GoOnWrite, and individual designer websites. This is the best option for new authors balancing budget and quality.

Custom covers ($300-1,000+) are designed specifically for your book by a cover designer you hire. You provide a brief — genre, subgenre, tone, comparable titles — and the designer creates original artwork. This is the best option for authors who need specific imagery, branding consistency across a series, or a cover that precisely matches their vision.

AI-assisted covers are an emerging option. Tools like Midjourney and DALL-E can generate cover artwork, but the results typically need professional composition, typography, and finishing by a human designer. Pure AI covers are not yet consistently competitive with professional designs, though the gap is closing.

### What should you never do with your cover?

Design it yourself unless you are a trained graphic designer with genre expertise. Use clip art. Use generic stock photos without professional composition. Copy another author's cover design. Use fonts that do not match your genre's visual language. Put too much text on the cover. Ignore the thumbnail test — your cover must be readable and impactful at the 150-pixel height it displays in Amazon search results.

## Formatting and production tools

### What are the best formatting tools?

Vellum (Mac only, $249 one-time for ebook and print) produces the most beautiful formatting in the industry with minimal effort. If you own a Mac, Vellum is the standard recommendation.

Atticus ($147 one-time, cross-platform) is the leading alternative for Windows users and authors who want an all-in-one writing and formatting solution. The output quality is close to Vellum, and the tool is actively developed with frequent updates.

Kindle Create (free, from Amazon) handles basic ebook formatting adequately. It lacks the polish and design options of Vellum or Atticus but costs nothing and produces functional results.

For print-only formatting, Adobe InDesign is the professional standard but requires a subscription ($22/month) and significant learning. Most indie authors do not need InDesign — Vellum, Atticus, or even a well-configured Word document produces adequate print interiors.

## Keyword and metadata tools

### How do you research keywords?

Publisher Rocket ($97 one-time) is the dominant keyword research tool for KDP authors. It shows search volume estimates, competition scores, and earnings potential for Amazon keywords, and it suggests browse categories. The data is not perfect — Amazon does not share exact search volumes publicly — but it is the best available approximation and significantly better than guessing.

Amazon's own search bar is a free and underrated research tool. Start typing a genre term and note the autocomplete suggestions — these are real searches that real readers are making. Combine autocomplete research with competitor analysis (what keywords are successful comparable books using?) for a robust keyword strategy.

KDP's category selection tool and the ability to request additional categories through support are metadata levers that most authors underutilize. Research which categories your competitor bestsellers are listed in, and request those same categories for your book.

### What about ISBN and copyright?

ISBN is optional for KDP ebooks (Amazon assigns a free ASIN). For print, KDP offers a free ISBN, but it is tied to Amazon's distribution. If you want to distribute print copies through IngramSpark or other channels, purchase your own ISBN ($125 for one, $295 for ten from Bowker in the US).

Copyright registration is recommended but not required for publication. In the US, your work is copyrighted automatically upon creation. Formal registration with the Copyright Office ($65 online) provides additional legal protections and is required before filing an infringement lawsuit.

## Marketing and advertising tools

### What are the essential marketing tools?

Amazon Ads (KDP Advertising) is the primary paid marketing channel. It integrates directly with KDP, targets readers already shopping for books, and provides performance data that allows optimization. Start with $5-10 per day and scale based on results.

An email service provider — ConvertKit, MailerLite, or Mailchimp — is essential for building and maintaining your reader list. MailerLite offers a generous free tier (up to 1,000 subscribers) that is sufficient for new authors.

BookFunnel ($20-150/year depending on plan) handles reader magnet delivery — when a reader signs up for your email list, BookFunnel delivers the free ebook to their device. It also manages ARC distribution and group promotions.

Social media is optional but increasingly important. BookTok (TikTok), Bookstagram (Instagram), and Facebook reader groups are the three highest-impact platforms for book discovery. You do not need to be on all three — pick the one where your target readers spend the most time and focus there.

### What about promotional services?

BookBub Featured Deals remain the gold standard for promotional reach, but acceptance rates are low (roughly 10-20% of submissions). When accepted, a BookBub deal can generate hundreds to thousands of sales in a single day. Submit regularly — the more books in your catalog, the higher your acceptance rate.

Secondary promotional services — Freebooksy, Robin Reads, Bargain Booksy, Book Barbarian — provide smaller but still meaningful boosts. Running promotions through these services during a launch or a $0.99 sale can drive the initial velocity that triggers Amazon's algorithmic visibility.

## Operations and scaling

### What tools help you scale?

Tracking finances: a simple spreadsheet or QuickBooks Self-Employed ($15/month) to track income, expenses, and royalties across platforms.

Project management: Notion or Trello for managing your publishing calendar, tracking manuscripts in progress, and organizing production workflows.

Mileage and tax tracking (for US authors): self-publishing income is self-employment income. Track expenses, deductions, and quarterly tax obligations from day one. A CPA familiar with author income is a worthwhile investment once you are earning consistently.

Translation management: for authors expanding into multilingual publishing, a translation management workflow — tracking languages, translators, publication status, and keyword localization — becomes essential. Spreadsheets work at small scale. Dedicated project management tools work better as you grow.
