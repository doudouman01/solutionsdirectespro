# How to Build an ARC Team and Launch Your Romance Book Like a Pro

Every romance author faces the same terrifying moment: launch day. Your book goes live on Amazon, and for the first hours — sometimes the first days — it sits there with zero reviews, zero ranking, and zero social proof. A reader who finds it sees a book that nobody has validated. In a genre where readers rely heavily on reviews to make purchasing decisions, that empty review section is a death sentence for discoverability.

ARC teams solve this problem. An ARC (Advance Review Copy) team is a group of readers who receive your book before publication in exchange for an honest review posted on launch day or shortly after. A well-managed ARC team means your book launches with 10-30 genuine reviews already in place — reviews that signal to both readers and Amazon's algorithm that this book is real, vetted, and worth discovering.

Building an ARC team is not complicated. But doing it well — in a way that produces reliable, high-quality reviews without violating Amazon's terms of service — requires understanding the mechanics, the ethics, and the relationship dynamics involved.

## Understanding ARC teams

### What exactly is an ARC team and how does it work?

An ARC team is a curated group of readers who agree to read your book before its release date and post an honest review on Amazon (and optionally on Goodreads, BookBub, or other platforms) within a specified window — typically launch day through the first week.

The exchange is simple: they get a free copy of your book before anyone else. You get reviews that provide social proof at the moment your book needs it most. The reviews must be honest — this is not a paid review service, and ARC readers are not obligated to leave positive reviews. They are obligated only to read the book and share their genuine opinion.

Most ARC teams operate through email (you send the ARC as an EPUB or MOBI file), through BookFunnel (a service designed specifically for ARC distribution), or through a private Facebook group or Discord server where you share files and coordinate review timing.

### Is it legal? Does Amazon allow ARC reviews?

Yes. Amazon's Terms of Service explicitly permit reviews from readers who received free copies, provided the reviewer discloses that they received the book for free and the review reflects their honest opinion. The standard disclosure is a simple line at the end of the review: "I received an advance copy of this book in exchange for an honest review."

What Amazon prohibits is incentivized reviews — offering payment, gifts, or reciprocal reviews in exchange for positive reviews. The distinction is critical: free book in exchange for an honest review (any rating) is permitted. Free book in exchange for a five-star review is not. Never instruct your ARC readers to leave a specific rating, and never make continued membership in your ARC team conditional on positive reviews.

## Building your ARC team from scratch

### Where do you find your first ARC readers?

For new authors without an existing audience, the initial ARC team comes from five sources.

First, your personal network — friends, family, and acquaintances who genuinely read romance. The operative word is "genuinely." A review from someone who does not read romance will read as inauthentic and may actually hurt your credibility. Only recruit people who would plausibly buy your book if it appeared in their Amazon recommendations.

Second, romance reader Facebook groups. Many groups (search for "ARC readers romance" or "romance book reviewers") exist specifically to connect authors with ARC readers. Post your book's cover, blurb, and subgenre/trope information, and interested readers will volunteer. These readers are experienced — they understand the process and deliver reliable reviews.

Third, BookTok and Bookstagram. Romance book communities on TikTok and Instagram are enormous, and many content creators actively seek ARCs for review content. Reach out to smaller accounts (1,000-10,000 followers) who review in your subgenre — they are more likely to respond and more likely to actually read and review your book than larger accounts who are overwhelmed with requests.

Fourth, BookSirens and similar ARC matching services. These platforms connect authors with verified reviewers. The cost is typically $20-50 per book launch, and the service handles distribution and follow-up. For new authors, this can supplement a small organic ARC team.

Fifth, your email list — if you have been building one through a reader magnet (a free novella or short story offered in exchange for an email signup). Even a small list of 50-100 subscribers can yield 10-20 reliable ARC readers.

### How many ARC readers do you need?

The math is straightforward. Not every ARC reader will actually read the book and post a review. Industry averages suggest a 40-60% completion rate for ARC teams — meaning that out of every 10 people who receive your ARC, 4-6 will post a review.

For a launch target of 15-20 reviews (a strong foundation for a new release), you need approximately 30-40 ARC readers. For a more aggressive target of 25-30 reviews, aim for 50-60 ARC readers. These numbers assume a well-managed team — the completion rate drops significantly if you do not send reminders and follow up.

Start with whatever number you can realistically recruit. Even five reviews on launch day is dramatically better than zero. You can build the team larger with each subsequent release as your readership grows.

### How do you vet ARC readers?

Not all ARC readers are equal. The best ARC readers are: active reviewers (check their Amazon review history — do they regularly review romance books?), readers in your specific subgenre (a historical romance reader reviewing your dark contemporary will not enjoy it and will leave a mediocre review), and reliable communicators (do they respond to messages? Do they meet deadlines?).

Create a simple application form that asks: what romance subgenres do you read most, approximately how many books do you review per month, and what is your Amazon reviewer profile link. This filters out casual applicants and gives you a team of committed readers who are likely to follow through.

## Managing your ARC process

### When should you send ARCs?

Send ARCs 3-4 weeks before your publication date. This gives readers enough time to read the book comfortably without rushing, while keeping the experience fresh enough that writing a review does not feel like a chore.

Include a clear, friendly email with: the book file (EPUB and MOBI for maximum device compatibility, or a BookFunnel link), a one-paragraph reminder of what the book is about, the publication date, a specific request for when to post the review (ideally launch day or within the first three days), and a reminder to include the ARC disclosure in their review.

Two days before launch, send a gentle reminder with the Amazon link where they can post their review. On launch day, send a final "the book is live!" message with a direct link to the review page. This three-touch communication sequence (send, remind, launch) maximizes completion rates.

### How do you handle negative ARC reviews?

With grace. Negative reviews from ARC readers are actually valuable in two ways. First, they provide credibility — a book with nothing but five-star reviews looks suspicious to savvy readers. A mix of ratings (mostly 4-5 stars with an occasional 3) signals authentic reviews and actually increases buyer confidence.

Second, constructive negative feedback from ARC readers — received before your wider launch — gives you the opportunity to fix issues. If multiple ARC readers mention a pacing problem in the second act, you can revise before the general public reads the book. Think of your ARC team as your last quality control checkpoint.

Never argue with an ARC reviewer, never ask them to change their rating, and never remove someone from your ARC team because they left a three-star review. These behaviors destroy trust and will cost you ARC readers far faster than a single mediocre review ever could.

## The launch sequence

### What does a professional romance launch look like?

A professional launch coordinates multiple channels simultaneously to create maximum first-week velocity. Here is the full sequence:

Four weeks before launch: distribute ARCs. Set up preorder on Amazon. Share cover reveal on social media. Begin building anticipation with teaser quotes and aesthetic posts.

Two weeks before launch: send ARC reminder. Submit to promotional newsletter services if running a launch deal. Prepare your email blast. Schedule social media posts for launch week.

One week before launch: final ARC reminder. Confirm your launch-day email is ready. Prepare any paid advertising (Amazon Ads, Facebook Ads) to go live on launch day.

Launch day: send email blast to your full list. Post across all social platforms — not just "my book is out" but content that communicates the emotional experience the book offers. Activate any promotional pricing or countdown deals. Monitor ARC reviews appearing on Amazon throughout the day.

First week post-launch: engage with every review and social media mention. Run any planned advertising. If reviews and sales are strong, consider a small promotional push (BookBub Featured Deal submission, cross-promotion with other romance authors in your subgenre).

### Why do the first seven days determine everything?

Amazon's algorithm is velocity-sensitive. The books that sell and get read the most in their first seven days receive a disproportionate boost in organic recommendations — "customers also bought," "recommended for you," category ranking, and newsletter features. This visibility drives additional sales, which drives more visibility, creating a compounding cycle.

A book that sells 50 copies in its first week will typically outsell a book that sells 100 copies in its first three months — even though the second book sold twice as many total copies — because the concentrated velocity triggered algorithmic visibility that the slower book never received.

This is why ARC reviews, email blasts, social media pushes, and promotional pricing all need to hit in the same narrow window. Spreading your launch efforts over a month is like spreading a fire over a football field — nothing catches. Concentrating them into a single week is like focusing a magnifying glass — everything ignites.

## Growing your ARC team over time

### How do you turn readers into ARC team members?

Every book you publish is an ARC team recruitment opportunity. In your back matter, include an invitation: "Want to read my next book before anyone else? Join my ARC team and get advance copies in exchange for honest reviews." Link to a signup form or your email list.

Readers who loved your book are the best possible ARC candidates. They are already fans. They already know your voice, your subgenre, and your style. Their reviews will be informed and enthusiastic because they are reviewing something they chose to read, not something a stranger handed them.

Over the course of a series, your ARC team will grow naturally. By book three, most successful indie romance authors have ARC teams of 50-100+ readers, producing 25-50 launch-day reviews per book. That level of social proof makes every subsequent launch significantly easier than the one before.

### How do you keep your ARC team engaged between launches?

The best ARC teams feel like communities, not labor forces. Between launches, keep your team engaged with: exclusive first looks at upcoming covers, early access to blurbs and series announcements, polls on character names or plot decisions (light participation, not co-authoring), and occasional personal updates that make them feel like insiders.

A quarterly newsletter to your ARC team — separate from your general reader newsletter — maintains the relationship during the gaps between books. When launch day arrives, engaged ARC readers respond with enthusiasm rather than obligation.
