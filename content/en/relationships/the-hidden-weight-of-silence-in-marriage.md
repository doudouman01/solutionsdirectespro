# The Hidden Weight of Silence in Marriage: Why Women Stay and What It Costs

Emotional silence in marriage — the sustained suppression of needs, opinions, and identity to maintain peace — affects a significant proportion of women in long-term relationships. According to the National Domestic Violence Hotline, emotional abuse is present in approximately 35% of U.S. marriages, and the American Psychological Association reports that women in emotionally suppressive relationships are 2.5 times more likely to develop chronic health conditions than women in equitable partnerships. Yet most of these women never seek help, because the silence they carry does not leave visible marks.

This is not the comfortable silence of two people who know each other well enough to sit without speaking. It is the other kind — the silence that is a strategy, a survival tool, a second skin worn so long that the woman underneath has almost forgotten she exists.

## How Does the Silence Start in a Marriage?

### Is It Always Obvious When a Marriage Becomes Emotionally Unsafe?

No, and that is precisely why it is so dangerous. It rarely starts with something dramatic. It starts with a comment that cuts a little too deep. A reaction that seems disproportionate. A door that slams hard enough to make the children look up from their plates. And then the recalibration — the unconscious, almost automatic adjustment that women learn to make in the space between one breath and the next.

According to Dr. John Gottman, founder of the Gottman Institute and one of the leading researchers on marital stability, the most reliable predictor of divorce is not the presence of conflict but the presence of contempt — and contempt often begins with precisely these small, cutting moments that accumulate over years. Gottman's research, based on over 40 years of longitudinal studies, shows that couples who engage in a pattern he calls "stonewalling" — where one partner withdraws emotionally — have an 85% chance of divorcing within 5 years.

### What Does Self-Silencing Actually Look Like?

The adjustments are small, individually insignificant. Do not say that. Do not wear that. Do not laugh that way. Do not bring it up. But they accumulate the way snow accumulates — flake by flake, gram by gram — until the weight is enough to collapse a roof.

Psychologist Dana Crowley Jack coined the term "self-silencing" in her 1991 research published in *Silencing the Self: Women and Depression*. Her Self-Silencing Scale, now used in over 200 peer-reviewed studies, measures the degree to which women suppress their authentic thoughts and feelings to maintain relationships. According to her research, women who score high on self-silencing are 3 to 4 times more likely to develop clinical depression than those who do not.

## What Does Silence Do to the Body?

### Can Emotional Suppression Cause Physical Symptoms?

Yes, and the research is unambiguous. Women who live in sustained states of hypervigilance — whose nervous systems never fully leave fight-or-flight — develop physical symptoms that mirror their emotional reality. According to a landmark study published in *Psychosomatic Medicine*, chronic emotional suppression increases cortisol levels by an average of 20%, which over time contributes to hypertension, immune dysfunction, and cardiovascular disease.

Common physical manifestations include jaw pain from clenching, chronic back tension from bracing, digestive disorders from a gut that has forgotten how to function in peacetime, and insomnia from a brain that cannot stop scanning for threats. According to Dr. Bessel van der Kolk, author of *The Body Keeps the Score* and one of the world's leading trauma researchers, these symptoms are frequently misdiagnosed as anxiety disorders, depression, irritable bowel syndrome, or fibromyalgia — labels that treat the symptom while ignoring the cause.

The body does not forget. The body is the most honest record keeper a woman has — more honest than memory, more honest than the narrative she has constructed to make her life bearable.

## Why Do Women Stay in Silent Marriages?

### Is It Really as Simple as "Just Leave"?

No. When outsiders learn that a woman stayed in a difficult marriage for twenty, thirty, forty years, the question is almost always: why did she not leave? It is the wrong question.

According to the Institute for Women's Policy Research, economic dependence is the primary reason women stay in unhappy marriages — 73% of women who remain cite financial insecurity as a major factor. But the barriers extend far beyond money. According to research published in the *Journal of Family Violence*, the average woman in an emotionally abusive relationship makes 7 attempts to leave before successfully separating. The barriers include social pressure (particularly in religious or rural communities where divorce carries stigma), psychological trauma bonds (a well-documented neurological phenomenon where intermittent reinforcement creates attachment to the source of harm), fear of losing custody, lack of family support, and the absolute certainty that no one will believe her because he is so charming in public.

The right question is not why she stayed. The right question is what it cost her to stay. And the answer is almost always the same: everything she was before the silence began.

## Does Silence in Marriage Affect the Next Generation?

### Do Children Learn Silence From Their Parents?

Yes, and this may be the most devastating consequence. According to research published in the *Journal of Marriage and Family*, children raised in households with high levels of parental emotional suppression are 60% more likely to replicate those patterns in their own adult relationships. Daughters learn from their mothers what a marriage looks like. Sons learn what is permissible.

The patterns replicate themselves across generations with the precision of a genetic code — not because the children want to repeat them, but because the patterns are the only template they have. According to the American Association for Marriage and Family Therapy, intergenerational transmission of relational patterns is one of the most robust findings in family systems research.

Breaking the cycle requires something extraordinary: a woman who decides, at some point in the long arc of her endurance, that the truth matters more than the performance. That her story deserves to be told — not for revenge, not for sympathy, but because silence is a poison that does not stop at the person who swallows it. It seeps into the soil. It feeds the roots. It blooms in the next generation until someone is brave enough to dig it up.

## Why Do We Need Stories About Silence?

Fiction matters in this space — not the fiction of escape, but the fiction of recognition. The novels that hold up a mirror and show a woman her own face, her own silence, her own impossible arithmetic of survival. The novels that say: I see you. I know what you carried. And I know what it cost.

The best Southern family sagas understand this implicitly. They know that the real drama is not in the catastrophic events but in the quiet spaces between them — the kitchen table at one in the morning, the garden tended with a devotion that is really a form of prayer, the letter written and never sent, the truth buried so deep that only the roses know where it is.

These stories do not offer easy resolutions. They offer something better: the dignity of being witnessed.
