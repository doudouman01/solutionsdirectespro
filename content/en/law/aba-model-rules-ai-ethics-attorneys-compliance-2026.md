# ABA Model Rules and AI: The Complete Ethics Compliance Guide for Attorneys Using Artificial Intelligence in 2026

A federal judge sanctioned an attorney for submitting a brief containing six fabricated case citations generated by ChatGPT. The attorney didn't verify the citations. The opposing counsel did. The judge's order was scathing, public, and career-defining — in the worst possible way.

This case became the cautionary tale for AI in legal practice. But it represents only one of many ethical pitfalls that attorneys face when integrating AI into their workflow. Confidentiality breaches. Competence failures. Supervision gaps. Billing improprieties. Disclosure obligations.

The ABA Model Rules weren't written with AI in mind. But they apply to AI with remarkable precision — because they were written to govern the ethical obligations of attorneys regardless of the tools they use. Understanding how these rules apply to AI isn't a future concern. It's a present requirement.

---

## Rule 1.1 — Competence

**The rule:** An attorney must provide competent representation, which requires the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation reasonably necessary for the representation.

**The AI application:** Competence now includes technological competence. Comment 8 to Rule 1.1 states that lawyers must "keep abreast of changes in the law and its practice, including the benefits and risks associated with relevant technology."

**What this means in practice:**

An attorney who uses AI tools without understanding how they work — their capabilities, limitations, error patterns, and failure modes — is incompetent in the same way an attorney who conducts legal research without understanding how to use a legal database is incompetent.

**Minimum competence requirements for AI use:**

- Understanding what AI can and cannot do reliably in legal practice
- Knowing that AI can generate false citations, incorrect legal analysis, and plausible-sounding but wrong conclusions
- Recognizing when AI output requires additional verification
- Understanding the specific AI tools you use — not AI in general, but the specific products you rely on
- Staying current with developments in legal AI technology and ethics guidance

**What competence does NOT require:**

- Understanding the technical architecture of AI systems
- Being able to build AI tools
- Knowing how neural networks process language
- Understanding the mathematics behind machine learning

You don't need to be an AI engineer. You need to be an informed user — the same way you need to understand how Westlaw works without understanding its database architecture.

## Rule 1.6 — Confidentiality

**The rule:** An attorney shall not reveal information relating to the representation of a client unless the client gives informed consent or the disclosure is impliedly authorized.

**The AI application:** When you input client information into an AI tool, you are potentially disclosing that information to the entity operating the tool. This creates three distinct confidentiality concerns:

**Data transmission.** Information entered into a cloud-based AI tool is transmitted to and processed by third-party servers. Unless the tool provider is operating under a confidentiality agreement or appropriate data protection framework, this transmission may constitute unauthorized disclosure.

**Data retention.** Many AI tools retain user inputs for model training, quality improvement, or other purposes. Client information retained by an AI provider is information outside the attorney's control — a continuing confidentiality exposure.

**Data security.** AI tool providers may not maintain the same security standards as legal technology providers. Data breaches at an AI provider could expose client information.

**Practical compliance:**

- **Never input client-identifiable information** into general-purpose AI tools without appropriate safeguards
- **De-identify before you prompt** — remove names, dates, case numbers, and other identifying details
- **Review data policies** — understand how each AI tool processes, retains, and potentially shares data
- **Use approved tools** — if your firm has vetted specific AI tools for confidentiality compliance, use those
- **Consider enterprise agreements** — enterprise AI plans often include data protection provisions, non-retention clauses, and confidentiality commitments that consumer plans do not

## Rule 5.3 — Supervision of Non-Lawyers

**The rule:** A lawyer having direct supervisory authority over a non-lawyer shall make reasonable efforts to ensure that the person's conduct is compatible with the professional obligations of the lawyer.

**The AI application:** AI is not a lawyer. It's not even a paralegal. It's a tool that generates text based on patterns in its training data. The supervision obligation that applies to paralegals, law clerks, and legal assistants applies with equal — arguably greater — force to AI.

**What supervision means for AI output:**

- Every AI-generated document must be reviewed by a qualified attorney before filing, sending, or delivering
- Review must be substantive, not cursory — the attorney must actually read and evaluate the content
- Citation verification is a mandatory part of review
- Legal analysis must be independently evaluated for accuracy and applicability
- Factual assertions must be confirmed against the record

**The associate analogy:** Treat AI output the way you'd treat a first draft from a first-year associate — talented but unreliable. You wouldn't file a brief without reading it because an associate wrote it. You wouldn't send a contract to a client without reviewing it because a paralegal prepared it. AI deserves the same scrutiny.

## Rule 3.3 — Candor Toward the Tribunal

**The rule:** A lawyer shall not knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal, offer evidence the lawyer knows to be false, or fail to disclose directly adverse authority.

**The AI application:** Submitting AI-generated legal citations without verification violates Rule 3.3 — because the attorney either knows or should know that AI can generate fabricated citations.

After the well-publicized sanctions cases, no attorney can credibly claim ignorance of AI's tendency to hallucinate citations. Using AI-generated citations without verification is not an innocent mistake. It's a failure of professional responsibility.

**The verification standard:**

Every citation generated by AI must be:
1. Confirmed to exist in an authoritative legal database
2. Read to verify the holding matches the attributed holding
3. Checked for negative treatment
4. Confirmed as relevant to the legal issue presented

This is not optional. It's not "best practice." It's the minimum standard of professional conduct.

## Rule 1.4 — Communication

**The rule:** A lawyer shall reasonably consult with the client about the means by which the client's objectives are to be pursued and keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter.

**The AI application:** Some jurisdictions now require disclosure to clients that AI tools are being used in their representation. Even where not required, disclosure is best practice for several reasons:

- **Transparency builds trust.** Clients who learn after the fact that AI was used may feel deceived — even if the work product was excellent.
- **Informed consent.** If AI use involves any transmission of client information (even de-identified), the client should understand and consent to that process.
- **Billing implications.** If AI reduces the time required for a task, the client may expect those efficiencies to be reflected in their bill.

**Recommended practice:** Include AI disclosure language in your engagement letter. Brief and straightforward: "Our firm uses artificial intelligence tools to enhance the efficiency and quality of certain legal tasks, including research, document drafting, and analysis. All AI-generated work product is reviewed and approved by a licensed attorney. Client information is protected in accordance with our confidentiality obligations."

## Rule 1.5 — Fees

**The rule:** A lawyer's fee shall be reasonable.

**The AI application:** AI creates billing ethics questions that the profession is still working through:

- If a task that previously took five hours now takes one hour with AI, is it ethical to bill five hours?
- If AI generates a first draft in minutes, can the attorney bill at their standard hourly rate for the review time?
- Should AI-related efficiency savings be passed to the client?

**The emerging consensus:**

- Billing for AI-generated work at the same rate as manual work without disclosure is ethically problematic
- The value of the work product — not the time spent — is the appropriate measure of a reasonable fee
- Alternative fee arrangements (flat fees, value-based pricing) align better with AI-enhanced practice than hourly billing
- Transparency about AI use and its impact on billing is essential

## State Bar Ethics Opinions

State bars across the country have issued formal opinions on AI. While the specifics vary, common themes emerge:

**Competence is universal.** Every state that has addressed AI emphasizes the duty of technological competence. Attorneys must understand AI tools before using them.

**Confidentiality is paramount.** Every opinion emphasizes the obligation to protect client information when using AI tools.

**Verification is mandatory.** Every opinion addresses the obligation to verify AI-generated legal analysis and citations.

**Disclosure varies.** Some states require client disclosure of AI use; others recommend it. The trend is toward requiring it.

**Supervision is non-delegable.** The attorney's review obligation cannot be satisfied by assuming AI is correct.

## Building an Ethical AI Framework for Your Practice

### Step 1: Develop an AI use policy

Document which AI tools are approved for use, what types of information can be input, what review protocols must be followed, and what documentation is required.

### Step 2: Train every attorney and staff member

Everyone who touches AI must understand the ethical obligations. Training should cover confidentiality, verification, supervision, disclosure, and billing.

### Step 3: Implement verification protocols

Create standardized citation verification checklists. Build verification into the workflow — not as an afterthought, but as a required step before any AI-generated content leaves the firm.

### Step 4: Update engagement letters

Include AI disclosure language in all engagement letters and retainer agreements.

### Step 5: Monitor and update

Ethics guidance on AI is evolving rapidly. Assign responsibility for monitoring new opinions, rule changes, and case law developments — and updating your firm's practices accordingly.

---

## The Bottom Line

The ABA Model Rules haven't changed. What's changed is the technology they apply to. AI is a powerful tool that makes attorneys more efficient, more thorough, and more competitive. But it doesn't change a single ethical obligation. The duty of competence, the obligation of confidentiality, the responsibility of supervision, and the requirement of candor all apply — fully, completely, and without exception.

The attorneys who thrive with AI in 2026 are those who embrace its capabilities while respecting the ethical framework that defines the profession.
